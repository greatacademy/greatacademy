# Chinese Curriculum

> English | [简体中文](chinese-curriculum.zh-CN.md)

This document specifies the design of the Chinese subject: its sources, its audiences, its knowledge graph, its courses for native, heritage and foreign-language learners, the size of each course in topics, knowledge points and problems, how it teaches and assesses characters, reading, writing, classical texts and speech, and what the core needs for it. It applies the method of [The Math Academy Way](the-math-academy-way.md), runs on the core described in [Content](content.md) and [Learning Engine](learning-engine.md), and reuses what [English Curriculum](english-curriculum.md) built: themed units, passages, recordings, judged writing and the voice service. The first slice of §15, native grades 3 to 6, is built; §15 gives the order of work.

---

## 1. Principles

1. **One subject, one graph.** Native, heritage and foreign-language learners share one graph, as in English. Courses are views over it, each with its own foundations and mastery floor ([Content §6](content.md#6-courses)).
2. **Follow the national standards.** Native courses follow the Ministry of Education's curriculum standards for compulsory and senior secondary Chinese; foreign-language courses follow the national standard for international Chinese education (§2). Where a standard gives a number, such as characters known by the end of grade 2, the course meets it.
3. **The character is the unit of literacy.** Chinese is read and written in characters, which are learned by their parts. Recognizing a character and writing it are separate skills, taught at different rates, as the standards require (§5).
4. **Courses follow levels, not skills.** A grade or a level teaches characters, words, grammar, reading, writing, listening and speaking together in themed units (§8).
5. **Memorized texts are reviewed like everything else.** The standards require the recitation of 135 classical poems and passages in grades 1 to 9 and 72 in senior secondary school. Spaced repetition suits them better than anything else in the subject (§7).
6. **Deterministic judgment wherever possible.** Rules judge characters, pinyin, words, grammar, punctuation and recited lines. AI judges only open writing and speech, after calibration (§11).
7. **Never copy textbooks.** Passages are original or in the public domain in every first market; textbook texts, exercises and word lists are never copied (§13).

## 3. Audiences

| | Native learners | Heritage learners | Foreign-language learners |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Who | Children of families that follow mainland schooling abroad, and adults | Children who speak Chinese at home but read little, common in the United States, Canada, Australia and Singapore | Learners of Chinese as a foreign language, children and adults |
| Already have | Speech in Mandarin, a large spoken vocabulary | Speech, often good listening, sometimes a dialect | Often literacy in another language, if adults |
| Main gaps | Characters, written language, classical texts, writing | Characters and written language; spoken vocabulary narrower than a native child's | Tones, speech, characters, vocabulary size, grammar |
| Courses | Grades 1 to 12 (§8.1) | Literacy bridges that join the grade or level courses (§8.2) | Levels 1 to 9 (§8.3) |
| Explanations in | Chinese | Chinese, with English available | The interface language; Chinese from level 4 |

Heritage learners are named separately, which English does not need: the diagnostic finds their listening far ahead of their reading, and the graph lets them learn characters for words they already say, without the beginner's dialogues of a foreign-language course.

Mainland China is closed to minors by an earlier decision ([Minors' Data](minors-data.md)), so native courses serve families abroad and adults. Hong Kong and Taiwan teach in traditional characters with their own curricula; those curricula are not followed here, and traditional characters are read in the third heritage bridge (§5.4).

## 4. What Differs from English and Mathematics

| Idea | In Chinese |
| :--- | :--- |
| Knowledge graph and frontier | Strong order in pinyin, strokes, components and characters: a character's components come before it. Weaker order in reading and literature, as in English |
| Vocabulary in batches | Applies twice: characters and words are both learned in batches (§5, §6) |
| Spaced repetition | Characters, words and recited texts. Recitation is where it is strongest (§7) |
| Fractional implicit repetition | A word encompasses its characters, a recited poem its characters, a composed sentence its grammar |
| Automaticity | Character recognition and reading speed: the standard asks for 300 characters a minute of silent reading by grade 6 and 500 by grade 9 |
| New input | Handwriting by strokes, pinyin with tones, typed Chinese (§10) |
| Reading coverage | Text has no spaces, so coverage is counted in characters and segmented words (§9) |
| Classical language | A strand of its own, from grade 3 to the end of school (§7) |

## 5. Characters

### 5.1 Recognizing and writing

The standard sets cumulative targets, which the grade courses meet:

| End of | Characters recognized | Characters written | Recited texts in the stage | Outside reading in the stage |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Grade 2 | about 1,600 | about 800 | 50 | 50,000 characters |
| Grade 4 | about 2,500 | about 1,600 | 50 | 400,000 characters |
| Grade 6 | about 3,000 | about 2,500 | 60 | 1,000,000 characters |
| Grade 9 | about 3,500 | (writing is to be correct and neat; no further number) | 80 | 2,600,000 characters |

Recognizing and writing are separate topics. A **recognition topic** is a batch of 12 characters: it links each one's form to its sound and to words the learner knows, which is how a child who speaks the language learns to read. A **writing topic** is a batch of 10 characters that the learner already recognizes: tracing each character in its words, then writing it from its pinyin with nothing to trace, stroke by stroke on the pad, judged against the standard medians of its strokes. A writing topic has its characters' recognition topics as prerequisites, and follows the writing topic before it. The native courses write the characters in the order they are read, so that a child writes what it read a while before; the counts follow the standard: 800 by grade 2, 1,600 by grade 4, 2,500 by grade 6 and 3,000 by grade 9, in 300 topics.

Foreign-language learners need meaning as well as sound, so their **character topics** link form, sound and meaning in batches of 10, ordered by the levels of GF 0025-2021. Their writing topics work as the native ones, ten characters a topic traced and then written from memory, but take the characters in the levels' teaching order and in the levels' words: 1,200 by level 9, as the handwriting list of the standard asks, in 120 topics (§8.3). A character with fewer than three such words is written from its reading and meaning as well.

### 5.2 Order: components first

The standard asks that the 300 basic characters of its appendix 4 be learned first: they are simple, frequent and most of them are parts of other characters. The graph does the same. Strokes and stroke order come first; then the basic characters; then the common components and radicals; then characters built from known parts, grouped where possible by a shared part, such as 河、湖、海、洋 by 氵. A character's components are prerequisites of its batch, so the frontier never asks for 想 before 相 and 心.

Until component data is chosen (§5.3), the recognition batches of grades 1 to 6 follow the order of the tables of appendix 5: 12 consecutive characters a batch, dealt in turn to the two grades of a stage, each batch a prerequisite of the grade's next. Grades 1 and 2 are the exception, since the tables are in the order of pinyin: their characters are ordered by frequency, the 900 characters of levels 1 to 3 first in the levels' order, with those of grade 1's words among level 1's, and grade 1 takes the first half of the batches, so that a child first reads 你, 我, 是 and 的. Of table one, 1,600 characters go to grades 1 and 2 and the other 900 to grades 3 and 4; of table two, 500 go to grades 5 and 6. Reviewers who teach Chinese check the split.

### 5.3 Data

Each character carries its standard form, its readings, its components, its stroke count and stroke order, its tier in the *Table of General Standard Chinese Characters* and its levels in the two standards. This is a subject's data, like the lexicon of English (§12). Stroke geometry for the handwriting checker is the medians of Make Me a Hanzi, under the Arphic Public License (§13).

### 5.4 Traditional characters

The courses are in simplified characters, the national standard. Traditional characters matter in Hong Kong and Taiwan, two of the first markets, but the mapping is not one to one (发 is both 發 and 髮), so converting a course is not a display setting. They are read instead in the third heritage bridge, in topics of their own after its topic of spoken and written Chinese: single traditional characters and words by their simplified forms; characters whose component is simplified the same way in every character, such as 語, 讀 and 談; and the traditional form a word needs where one simplified character stands for several, told apart by the word it is in. The forms are those Hong Kong and Taiwan share; a word the two write differently, such as 復習 and 複習, is left out.

## 6. Words, Grammar and Punctuation

- **Words** come in batches of 10 by theme or by a shared morpheme, as in English. Idioms (成语) come in batches of 8 from grade 3, with their meaning, their usual errors and their use in a sentence.
- **Grammar for native learners** makes explicit what they already use, within the list of the standard's appendix 3: twelve word classes, five phrase structures, six sentence parts, seven kinds of complex sentence, twelve punctuation marks and eight rhetorical devices. It is taught through use: correcting faulty sentences (病句), changing sentence forms and choosing connectives, as the standard asks, not by defining terms.
- **Grammar for foreign-language learners** follows the grammar points of GF 0025-2021, 572 in all: measure words, aspect with 了, 过 and 着, complements, 把 and 被, and so on. Most are one topic each; a few small points share one.
- **Punctuation** follows GB/T 15834-2011, including the marks English does not have, such as 顿号 and 书名号.

The native grammar topics, punctuation, faulty sentences, connectives, sentence forms and rhetoric, teach each point with a sentence of their own: each topic opens with one instance worked through, and each knowledge point gives its rule, walks one sentence and names the mistake to watch for, such as a question word taken for a question, a long opening taken for a subject, or 只要 paired with 才. Each faulty sentence has its own note: what is wrong with it, why its correction is sound and why each wrong revision is not. A solution goes in steps, from the words beside a gap, the clauses, the doer of a 把 sentence or what a sentence does, to the answer, and checks it another way: by the test a mark or a device passes, by testing the correction as the faulty sentence was tested, or by changing a rewritten sentence back. Every wrong option is answered with its own reason.

The grammar topics of the levels are taught in the same way, in plain English for foreign-language learners. Each topic opens with its first gap worked through; each knowledge point works one item and ends with the commonest mistake of its items. A solution names the words beside the gap, says what the right word does and checks the sentence against the rule. A wrong option is compared with the right one word by word, and the first step of a sentence-choice solution names where the options differ in that item. Each wrong option gets the rule it breaks. For a wrong order, the reason names the kind of the word that moved and where words of that kind stand (an adverb or a word of negation before the verb, a preposition phrase before the verb, a measure word between the number and the noun, an end particle last), says that a word of two or more characters stays together, or, for dates and times, gives the order the topic teaches. For a word added, left out or put for another, the reason says what the words do, from a list of grammar words such as 不, 没, 了, 着, the conjunctions and the measure words, and why that fails in this sentence. Pairs that neither the list nor the meanings can tell apart, such as words of like meaning that differ in grammar or position (一样 and 同, 半 and 一半) or a word broken by a fragment (聊了一会儿天), have reasons written by hand. A fixed phrase is explained as a whole, never by its single characters. A word whose use changes with the sentence is explained by its use in that sentence: 也 after 哪怕 or 即使 says the result still holds, and 再 before an adjective and 也 says “however”. A reason written by hand for one sentence may take in the words around the difference, so the same two words can have different reasons in different sentences.

## 7. Classical Texts and Recitation

The compulsory standard recommends 135 classical poems and passages to recite, 75 in grades 1 to 6 and 60 in grades 7 to 9; the senior secondary standard recommends 72 more, 32 in classical prose and 40 poems, songs and lyrics. All of them are in the public domain.

- **One topic per recited text.** Its knowledge points ask for a line from the one before or after it, a line from a description of its meaning, and the correct writing of a line among versions with a wrong character. The review is the same retrieval, spaced by the engine, which is exactly what recitation needs. A text read aloud from memory is a spoken knowledge point, skipped without a microphone.
- **Distinct problems.** A short poem gives fewer than 30 distinct cues. These knowledge points are therefore an exception to the depth required of other rule-checked points, as the listening topics of English are: their items grow only with the text.
- **Classical language** (文言) has topics of its own from grade 5: content words in batches, the 18 common function words, sentence patterns such as the judgment, the passive, omission and inversion, ancient and modern meanings, borrowed characters and translation into modern Chinese. Passages are classical texts in the public domain. Recited classical passages begin earlier, in grade 3, each in the grade whose national textbook teaches it; the drills still begin in grade 5. Each drill topic opens by reading one sentence through; each knowledge point lists the usages it tells apart, each with how it is told in a sentence, walks one sentence through and names the mistake its wrong options record. A solution goes in steps, from where the word stands, or what the sentence needs, to the usage or meaning, and checks it by reading the whole sentence in modern Chinese beside another of the same usage, or by setting a translation against the original clause by clause; every wrong option is answered with its own reason.

## 8. Courses

A course is a grade or a level. Its units are themes, each with characters, words, grammar, reading, writing, listening and speaking (§8.4), and the engine chooses from the frontier as in English: themes wrap the content, they do not set its order.

### 8.1 Native Courses

One course per grade, `chinese-grade-1` to `chinese-grade-9`, and two for senior secondary school, `chinese-hs-required` and `chinese-hs-selective`, following the required and selective required courses of the senior standard. The standard's electives are not built as courses: it leaves them to each school, the university entrance examination does not test them, and their task groups are seminars and research on whole works and themes, which mastery practice does not check; such works are read outside the app, with a topic per book (§9). The Gaokao course is in §8.5.

| Course | What weighs most |
| :--- | :--- |
| Grades 1 and 2 | Characters first, then pinyin as a tool, as the 2024 revision of the national textbooks does; strokes and components; the dictionary; 1,600 characters recognized and 800 written; nursery rhymes and short poems; writing a sentence, then a few |
| Grades 3 and 4 | 2,500 and 1,600 characters; choosing a sense in the dictionary; words and idioms; paragraphs; narrative and expository reading; poems; the first short classical passages |
| Grades 5 and 6 | 3,000 and 2,500 characters; reading speed; classical passages; whole books; compositions of 400 to 500 characters |
| Grades 7 to 9 | 3,500 characters and semi-cursive writing; classical prose; argument and exposition; three whole books a year; a composition of 500 characters in 45 minutes |
| Senior secondary | The 18 task groups: whole-book reading, language, literature, critical reading, practical reading, classical texts, revolutionary and modern literature, foreign literature, scientific and cultural works |

Grades 1 and 2 are courses of their own, larger than in English because the standard asks for 1,600 characters by the end of grade 2 and Chinese takes a fifth of school time. Their lessons stay short, for a child learning with an adult nearby. There is no preschool course: the guidelines for children aged 3 to 6 ask for listening, speaking and readiness, not characters.

Every native course has a unit of reading aloud and whole books after its writing: a topic of reading the grade's own passages aloud, a few whole sentences at a time, heard by a speech service that checks the characters said and not the accent (§11), and from grade 3 to the senior required course a practice-only topic of the grade's whole book (§9). Grade 2 has a unit on using a dictionary, as the standard asks of grades 1 and 2: looking a character up by the order of pinyin, the capital letter and then the syllable, and by its radical and then the strokes left, asked of characters the learner already knows; grade 3 goes on to choosing the sense of a character or word in a dictionary (§18).

### 8.2 Heritage Courses

Three literacy bridges, `chinese-heritage-1` to `chinese-heritage-3`, take a learner who speaks Chinese from no reading to about 600, 1,500 and 2,500 characters. They are views over the native graph: the same recognition and writing topics, with the native word batches their passages use, ordered for an older learner, with explanations in Chinese or English and passages written for their age. Their own topics are few: written forms of words used only in speech, the differences between a dialect and Mandarin, register, and traditional characters (§5.4). After a bridge, the diagnostic places the learner in the grade course that matches their reading, or in levels 5 to 9. The third bridge covers the recognition of grades 1 to 4, so the grade 5 course builds on it as well as on grade 4.

### 8.3 Foreign-Language Courses

Levels follow GF 0025-2021, `chinese-level-1` to `chinese-level-9`. The standard keeps levels 7 to 9 together; the subject splits their lists into three courses of equal size by frequency, so that no course is three times the size of the others.

| 7 to 9 | 202 (1,110) | 1,200 (3,000) | 5,636 (11,092) | 148 (572) | 500 (1,200) |

New items per level, with the cumulative total in brackets. Level 1 begins with tones and the sounds a speaker of English or Japanese confuses, heard and said from recordings, then pinyin, then characters; the first characters are basic ones, as for native learners (§5.2). Practice follows the four strands of Nation, as in English.

The pinyin topics teach each rule with a syllable or word of their own: each opens with one instance worked through, and each knowledge point gives its rule, walks one instance and names the mistake its wrong options record, such as zh taken for z, an for ang, or 一 said yí before a tone that is not the fourth. A solution goes in steps, from the mark, the letters before the first vowel, the spelling rule or the tone after 一 and 不, to the answer, and checks it another way: against the four tones of ma, by putting the syllable back together, by undoing the spelling rule, against another word of the same tone change, or, for a word said aloud, by humming its pitch first. Every wrong option is answered with its own reason, a syllable heard included.

### 8.4 Units

| Part | In a unit on the market | Judged by |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Characters | A recognition batch and a writing batch (§5) | Rules |
| Words | A batch of 10: 买、卖、斤、便宜、贵 … | Rules |
| Grammar | One or two points the theme uses: measure words, 多少 and 几 | Rules |
| Reading | Graded passages on the theme (§9) | Rules |
| Listening | The unit's passages and dialogues heard without their text | Rules |
| Writing | Sentences, then a paragraph on the theme | Rules, then AI |
| Speaking | Saying the unit's words and sentences, reading aloud | Speech services |
| Conversation | A spoken scene, such as buying fruit at a stall | AI against a rubric |

Native units are knowledge units and genres; heritage and foreign-language units are themes of daily life, then of society and culture.

### 8.5 Exam Courses

Practice-only courses over topics already in the graph, with exam topics of their own for the question types: `chinese-hsk-1` to `chinese-hsk-6` and `chinese-hsk-7-9` for the new HSK, and `chinese-gaokao` for the university entrance examination, whose basis is level 4 of the senior standard. They follow the courses they test, and each HSK course follows the one before it as well, which holds the level before, so that the HSK courses make a route of their own after pinyin.

The papers of HSK 1 to 6 follow the structure the test owner published in December 2025: listening and reading at every level, and writing from HSK 2, which asks for characters from their pinyin at HSK 2 and 3, sentences to complete at HSK 3, sentences each with a given word and a short text at HSK 4, a story from four pictures and a text of at least 200 characters at HSK 5, and a practical text and an essay of opinion of at least 300 at HSK 6. Each course practises the items of its level that the graph's topics check, the paper's dialogues and talks heard and texts read, each with questions, written for the course in its levels' characters, the writing of its levels' characters by hand, at HSK 2 and 3 those characters written where their pinyin stands in a word and in a sentence, at HSK 3 the sentences to complete as sentences built from their parts, from HSK 4 the first part of the writing paper in a judged topic of its own, and at HSK 7–9, a test of 98 items in five parts, its chart, its essay and its written translation from English, each judged; the story of HSK 5 tells four scenes described in words until the course has pictures. Beyond words said after a recording and sentences read aloud, the speaking test has a topic of its own: at HSK 1 to 3 a question heard and answered aloud in a whole sentence, with a hint of what to answer, heard against the answers accepted; from HSK 4 the course's short passages read aloud a few whole sentences at a time. Retelling, describing a picture and answering at length are not practised: they need a free answer judged by a model, and a spoken topic is checked by rules. The levels the courses hold practise dialogues too: units 6 and 12 of every level end their listening with a topic of dialogues between a woman and a man, heard in two voices without their text. No question of the test is copied.

## 9. Reading Volume

Reading tasks work as in English: graded passages, offered when enough of their characters and words are known, in knowledge units, with a short check. Two things change.

- **Coverage is counted in characters and words.** A passage declares the characters and words it uses; the share of known running characters and of known segmented words are both computed, and a passage is offered when both reach their targets. The targets are parameters to calibrate from the pilot, as English's 98% is.
- **Segmentation is deterministic.** Passages are segmented at compile time by the lexicon, longest match first, with manual breaks where that is wrong. Segmentation is content, not a model, so it is the same everywhere.

The standard's outside reading, from 50,000 characters in grades 1 and 2 to 2,600,000 in grades 7 to 9, is far more than the subject can write. Passages cover the part that needs grading; whole books are read outside the app, in print, one a course from grade 3 to the senior required course: *Andersen's Fairy Tales*, *Ancient Chinese Myths*, *Journey to the West*, *Robinson Crusoe*, *Dawn Blossoms Plucked at Dusk*, *Souvenirs Entomologiques*, *Water Margin* and *Dream of the Red Chamber*, works in the public domain that the standard's reading lists name. Each has a practice-only topic of at least 30 questions of four answers written for the subject, on its story, its people and what it means, quoting none of its text at length.

## 10. Input and Checkers

`libs/subject-chinese` holds the checkers, pure like the others.

| Checker | Accepts |
| :--- | :--- |
| Choice and order | As in English |
| Pinyin | Tone marks or tone numbers, ü as v, spaces and apostrophes by GB/T 16159; the original tone is written, not the changed tone of sandhi |
| Characters | Typed Chinese compared after normalization; a variant form counts as wrong where the standard form is taught |
| Strokes | Handwriting as a sequence of strokes on screen, matched stroke by stroke against the standard order and shapes; each wrong stroke is reported where it happened |
| Recited lines | Exact characters and punctuation of the line, so that a wrong character is the error it is |
| Speech | Pronunciation assessment for zh-CN, with tones; as in English, intelligibility is judged, not accent, and low confidence asks for another recording |

The web and the apps need a handwriting pad and a pinyin keyboard with tones; typed Chinese uses the device's input method.

## 11. Writing and Speaking

The levels of English apply, with character counts instead of word counts.

| Level | In Chinese | Judged by |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| W1 Conventions | Wrong characters, punctuation, pinyin | Rules |
| W2 Sentences | Expanding and combining sentences, changing sentence forms, connectives | Rules for form, AI for meaning |
| W3 Revision | Correcting faulty sentences, ordering sentences, choosing the better word | Mostly rules |
| W4 Paragraphs | One move at a time | AI, against yes-or-no criteria |
| W5 Compositions | 记叙文, 说明文, 议论文 and practical writing, from 300 characters in grade 3 to the 800-character essay of the Gaokao | AI, against a rubric |

Judged writing needs calibration sets scored by teachers of Chinese, separate from those of English. Native grades 3 to 11 have two judged topics a grade, and the Gaokao course a practice-only essay and short answers on informational and literary texts and classical poems, judged against their passage, each with three yes-or-no criteria asked in English of a text in Chinese; the prompt `writing-zh-1` asks the judge to count characters and to answer in Simplified Chinese. Foreign-language levels 2 to 9 have two judged paragraph topics a level, after sentence building, with three criteria each, and the HSK courses from HSK 3 on practise them again in a writing topic of their own; the prompt `writing-zh-l2-1` asks the judge to count characters, to judge the text as the writing of a learner at the task's level, and to answer in plain English that quotes the learner's Chinese. Speaking follows S1 to S6 of English: listening to tones comes first for foreign-language learners; native learners practise reading aloud, not pronunciation: each native course reads its passages aloud in a topic whose pronunciation score is not asked for, so a regional accent costs nothing. Speeches, judged by what they say, would need a model, and a spoken topic is checked by rules, so they are not practised.

## 12. Content Model and Core Changes

The core stays subject-agnostic, but three of its assumptions are English's.

- **Tokenizing text.** `bodyWords` and `spokenWords` in `libs/content` split on letters and spaces, so a run of Chinese characters counts as one word. The core must take the subject's tokenizer: characters and segmented words for Chinese, as now for English.
- **Characters as data.** A subject may define characters, as English defines a lexicon: form, readings, components, strokes, tier and levels. The compiler checks that a batch's components are taught before it.
- **New inputs.** `strokes` and `pinyin` join the input kinds, with their checkers in `libs/subject-chinese` and their pads in the apps.

Everything else is reused: `t(en, zh)` with Chinese being learned written in both locales the same, item banks, passages, recordings, judged writing, conversations and the voice service. The engine needs no change: character and word batches are topics, and if the pilot shows that characters within a batch are forgotten at very different rates, the engine gains item-level memory then, as was decided for English words.

## 13. Sources of Content and Rights

- **Standards** are cited and summarized, never reproduced.
- **Character lists.** The *Table of General Standard Chinese Characters* was issued by the State Council; whether it, the lists of GF 0025-2021 and the appendices of the curriculum standards may be used as data is a question for counsel before content is written, as E0.5 asked for English's word sources.
- **Dictionary data.** CC-CEDICT is licensed CC BY-SA, which would make derived glosses share-alike, so glosses are written for the subject instead. Unihan, under the Unicode license, may supply readings and radicals.
- **Stroke data** for the handwriting checker: the medians of `graphics.txt` of Make Me a Hanzi, derived from two Arphic PL fonts under the Arphic Public License, which allows paid use. The 3,000 characters served are a modified version, kept under the same license with its text and published freely to anyone (`content/chinese/SOURCES.md`).
- **Texts.** Classical texts are in the public domain. A modern text is used only if it is in the public domain in every first market, checked per work; otherwise passages are written for the subject. Textbook texts and exercises are never copied.
- **Reference repository.** `cn-math-taxonomy` was read for orientation. It is licensed CC BY-NC 4.0, which forbids commercial use, so none of its data, questions or wording is taken.

## 16. Risks and Questions

- **Volume.** Some 4,200 topics are as much content as mathematics. Character and word batches are regular enough to generate from data, which is where tooling matters most.
- **Handwriting.** Judging strokes fairly for children's writing on a phone is new to the platform and needs testing on real devices with real children.
- **Tones and speech services.** Pronunciation assessment for Mandarin tones has to be tested on learners whose first language is English, Japanese and Korean, and on children.
- **Rights.** The character lists and stroke data may limit or delay what can be built (§13).
- **Fit with schools.** Families abroad may want the textbook's order, which the graph does not follow. The grade courses cover each grade's standard, not its textbook.
- **Exam dates.** The new HSK's dates come from secondary sources and are confirmed with the test owner first.

## 18. The Standards, Requirement by Requirement

A learner may leave the native courses for a school at any point, so each grade course teaches what the two curriculum standards require of its stage, in the proportions they give it, and nothing they do not ask for. This section maps every stage requirement (学段要求) of the compulsory standard, its six learning task groups and the task groups of the senior required and selective required courses against the courses, compares the recited texts with the standards' recommended lists, and sets out the topics that close the gaps. Requirements are summarized, not quoted (§13).

### 18.1 How the Map Reads

Each requirement is **covered** when topics or reading units practise it as the standard asks, **partly** when they practise some of it, and a **gap** when nothing does; the last column names the topics that fill it (§18.9). A few requirements are not mastery practice: handwriting with a brush and in semi-cursive script, silent reading posture, the volume of reading outside class, and the projects of organizing and inquiry and of interdisciplinary learning (school magazines, performances, surveys, research across subjects). They are done outside the app, as the electives are (§8.1), and §18.7 lists them. Silent reading speed, 300 characters a minute by grade 6 and 500 by grade 9, needs problems that time the reading, which the core does not have; it waits for that change.

Before this section, grade 3 had 104 topics: 80 character batches and dictionary topics, 8 recited texts, 8 word batches, 4 of grammar, 2 of writing, 1 of reading aloud and 1 whole book, besides three reading units of three passages. Grades 4 to 9 were alike. Characters and recitation were covered; reading comprehension, composition and spoken communication were not.

### 18.9 The New Topics

Five kinds of topic close the gaps, each written to the teaching standard from the start (an introduction with its own instance, explanations that teach with an instance and end with a warning, solutions in steps that end with a check, and choice solutions that say why each other option is wrong) and listed in `teaching-standard.ts`:

| Kind | Ids | Checker | Content |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Reading | `reading-<grade>-<kind>` | Choice, with why each wrong option is wrong | At least 10 passages a topic and 30 questions a knowledge point, written for the subject or in the public domain in every first market (classical prose and poems, classical vernacular novels, folk tales and fables retold for the subject, foreign works translated for it); the grade's reading units feed the banks. No textbook passage or exam question is used |
| Judged writing | `writing-<grade>-<kind>` | `writingGenerator`, three yes-or-no criteria in `libs/subject-chinese`, two knowledge points of two criteria each | Twelve tasks with a model answer each, lengths in characters from 20 in grade 1 to 800 in senior school |
| Writing by rules | `revising-<grade>`, `planning-7-outline` | Choice, typed characters, order | Faulty pieces of a learner's draft to correct; outlines to choose and order |
| Speaking | `speaking-<grade>-<kind>` | Choice by rules: the reply that suits the listener, the purpose and the occasion; the relay that keeps the whole message | Scenes written for the subject; in grades 1 and 2 the scene and the options are heard from recordings made ahead, and from grade 3 the scenes of listening and relaying are heard; until the recordings are made, what is said is shown as text |
| Language and inquiry | `pinyin-alphabet`, `dictionary-words`, `language-<grade>-<kind>`, `inquiry-<grade>-sources` | Choice, typed characters, pinyin | Characters and words of the grade |

Each topic has two knowledge points; titles are given in English and Chinese, and the knowledge points by what they ask where the title does not say it. Where none are named, a reading topic's first point asks what the passage says and its second how it says it, by the method its title names; a judged topic's two points each judge the piece by two of its three criteria, as in §11; and a speaking topic's first point asks for the fitting reply in scenes read and its second in scenes heard, both heard in grades 1 and 2.

A reading or speaking question is marked as a fact or an inference. A fact question (`=` in the data) has its answer in the words of the text, and its solution finds that sentence, or those places, and chooses by them. An inference question (`≈`) asks what a passage shows, means or feels, why it is written so, or what follows from a table or a rule: its solution names the places of the text the answer rests on, reasons from them (in the question's own reasoning, `%`, where one place does not show how), and checks the answer against the whole text and its ending, never claiming that the text says it word for word. The parser rejects an inference that rests on one place without its reasoning.
