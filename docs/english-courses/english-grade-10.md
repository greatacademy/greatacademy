# English, Grade 10

> This is Great Academy's own outline. Its design is in the [English curriculum](../english-curriculum.md) §6.3 and the [development plan](../english-development-plan.md) §E8. [简体中文](english-grade-10.zh-CN.md).

- **Course:** `english-grade-10`
- **Level:** Grade 10, for learners whose first language is English or who read English as well as a native speaker of their age
- **Prerequisite(s):** `english-grade-9`, or reading and writing literary analyses, scenes, narratives and arguments with sources at the level of grade 9
- **Language of explanation:** English
- **Size:** 9 units · 52 topics (10 existing, 42 new: 20 of vocabulary, 6 of grammar and mechanics, 7 of reading, 3 of writing checked by rules, 6 of writing judged by a model) · about 830 XP · 8 reading units of 66 passages (2 existing, 6 new) · 9 conversations (2 existing, 7 new)

## Aim

By the end of grade 10 a learner reads the speeches, poems, stories of ambition and war, writing on the natural world, news and texts on money and work, and satires of the grade on their own and writes about them in analytical essays. In speeches they see the rhetorical situation, the appeals to reason, character and feeling, and the devices that carry them; in poems they know the forms and meters and read an ambiguous line for more than one meaning; in stories they follow complex characters and their motives, a shift of tone and an extended metaphor. In the news they spot bias and numbers that mislead, and they read satire for its target and its exaggeration. They balance sentences with parallelism and antithesis, build periodic and cumulative sentences, use fragments and questions for effect, write some of whom and whose, choose the active or the passive and the main clause for emphasis, order words for emphasis and keep apart a sixth set of commonly confused words. They keep an objective tone, state a theme and show qualifications with evidence; and they write a rhetorical analysis, an analysis of a poem, a thematic analysis, a comparison of two texts, a descriptive essay on a place, an editorial, a letter of application and a satire. They know the words of rhetoric, argument and poetic form and device, of seven themes: ambition and tyranny, loyalty and guilt, war and memory, ecology and landscapes, journalism and the media, economics and careers, and satire and folly, and more Latin and Greek roots, the suffixes that form adjectives and two more sets of academic words.

## How to Read This Outline

Every unit is a theme and teaches four things together: the words of the theme, grammar and mechanics, reading skills, and writing. It ends with a reading unit on the theme and a conversation, except the last, which has a conversation but no reading unit. Topics marked *existing* are in the graph already, in the band course `english-grades-9-12`, and keep their ids and their own prerequisites; each new topic lists its prerequisites, which may lie in an earlier unit or an earlier grade. A new topic of words lists the words it teaches, none of which another batch of the native courses teaches; any other new topic says what it teaches and its knowledge points as steps. A judged writing topic names its task and the criteria of its rubric, marking the criteria that are new.

Strands: of the 52 topics, 22 teach words (42%), 8 grammar and mechanics (15%), 11 reading (21%), 3 writing checked by rules (6%) and 8 writing judged by a model (15%).

Depth: each new grammar, reading or rule-checked writing knowledge point has 30 to 48 items built from hand-written cores by templates; each word of a vocabulary batch appears in three sentences, so each vocabulary knowledge point has 30 items; each judged writing knowledge point has 12 tasks with a model; new reading units have 8 passages of 300 to 600 words, with three questions each. A batch of ten words earns about 15 XP, a word-study, grammar, reading or rule-checked writing topic about 14 and a judged writing topic about 24, and the existing topics keep their own XP, so the lessons of the grade come to about 830 XP.

Listening and speaking wait for audio and recording: until then a unit lists its conversation but does not offer it, and each unit says what it will gain.

The band course `english-grades-9-12` retires once `english-grade-9` to `english-grade-12` hold all of its topics. Its 37 topics are divided among the four grades by its units, each in exactly one, with its id unchanged: 11 in grade 9 (the writer's craft, tragedy and the stage, and sentence variety for effect), 10 in grade 10 (voices of persuasion and poems across time), 12 in grade 11 (rights and laws, a century of change, and science and society) and 4 in grade 12 (essays of experience). Its reading units and conversations go with their units.

## Units

### 1. Voices of Persuasion

**Words:** `words-of-rhetoric` *(existing)*; `words-of-argument-and-reasoning` Words of Argument and Reasoning (after `context-clues`): axiom, inference, corollary, syllogism, specious, fallacious, untenable, equivocate, caveat, conjecture.

**Grammar:** `periodic-and-cumulative-sentences` *(existing)*. `parallelism-and-antithesis` Parallelism and Antithesis (after `periodic-and-cumulative-sentences`): How balanced sentences set ideas side by side in the same grammatical form, and how antithesis sets opposites against each other for force. Steps: The Balanced Contrast; then Naming the Balance.

**Reading:** `rhetorical-situation`, `rhetorical-appeals`, `rhetorical-devices` *(existing)*.

**Writing:** `writing-a-rhetorical-analysis` *(existing)*.

**Reading unit:** `voices-of-persuasion` *(existing)*, 10 passages: The Bridge on Mill Street; A Letter on the Four-Day Week; We Who Plant Trees; The Case Against the Mall; To the Graduates; The Miracle Tea; The Right to Read; A Speech for the River; Two Reports on Homework; The Last Stand of the Old Theater.

**Conversation:** `speech-coaching-session` *(existing)*. Task: Present the opening of a speech you plan to give, then revise it after the coach asks you about your audience.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a famous speech and hearing its appeals and devices; delivering a short speech with one device on purpose.

### 2. Poems Across Time

**Words:** `words-for-poetic-form` *(existing)*; `words-for-poetic-devices` Words for Poetic Devices (after `context-clues`): synecdoche, caesura, metonymy, anaphora, assonance, consonance, chiasmus, apostrophe, volta, sibilance.

**Grammar:** `fragments-and-questions-for-effect` Fragments and Questions for Effect (after `rhetorical-devices`): When a skilled writer breaks the rules on purpose: a deliberate fragment for emphasis, and questions that are not asked for an answer. Steps: Deliberate or Accidental; then Questions That Do Not Ask. `word-order-for-emphasis` *(existing)*.

**Reading:** `poetic-forms-and-meter` *(existing)*. `ambiguity-and-interpretation` Ambiguity and Interpretation (after `how-a-theme-develops`, `unreliable-narrators`): When a text deliberately supports more than one reading, and how to judge which interpretations the evidence allows. **Writing:** `writing-a-poetry-analysis` *(existing)*.

**Reading unit:** `poems-across-time` *(existing)*, 8 passages: The Sonnet: A Little Song; The Ballad: A Story in Song; The Haiku: A Moment Held Still; The Elegy: Words for the Dead; The Ode: A Song of Praise; Breaking the Line; Hearing the Beat; Two Poems, One River.

### 3. Ambition and Power

**Words:** `words-of-ambition-and-tyranny` Words of Ambition and Tyranny (after `context-clues`): megalomania, hegemony, oligarchy, imperious, ruthless, depose, abdicate, insatiable, overweening, benevolent; `words-of-loyalty-and-betrayal` Words of Loyalty and Betrayal (after `context-clues`): turncoat, collude, perfidy, treachery, duplicity, subterfuge, staunch, fickle, renegade, confidant; `words-of-guilt-and-conscience` Words of Guilt and Conscience (after `context-clues`): compunction, contrition, culpable, complicit, absolve, exonerate, penance, vindicate, recant, expiate.

**Grammar:** `some-of-whom-and-whose` Some of Whom, Most of Which and Whose (after `prepositions-before-relative-pronouns`): Relative clauses that begin with a word of quantity, such as *most of which*, and the possessive *whose*, used for things as well as people. Steps: A Quantity and a Relative Pronoun; then Whose for People and Things.

**Reading:** `complex-characters-and-motives` Complex Characters and Their Motives (after `tragic-heroes-and-flaws`, `dynamic-and-static-characters`): How a complex character acts from mixed or hidden motives, and how a reader infers a motive from what the character does, says and hides. Steps: What Drives the Character; then Mixed Motives.

**Writing:** `stating-a-theme` Stating a Theme (after `how-a-theme-develops`, `an-analytical-thesis`): Writing a theme as a complete, arguable idea about life that the text supports, rather than a single word, a plot summary or a command. Steps: Is It a Theme Statement?; then The Stronger Theme Statement. `writing-a-thematic-analysis` Writing a Thematic Analysis (judged by a model; after `stating-a-theme`, `writing-a-literary-analysis-essay`): Write a short analysis that states the theme of a fable or story as a complete idea and shows, with quoted details, how the text develops it. Criteria: `g10-theme-statement` *(new)*, `g10-theme-development` *(new)*, `g10-theme-quotes` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `ambition-and-power` Ambition and Power *(new)*, 8 passages: The Crown He Took; The Ides of March; The General Who Changed Sides; The Emperor of the Wall; The King Who Gave Up the Throne; The Blame That Fell on Sam; The Tallest Tower; Telling the Truth to Heal.

**Conversation:** `ambition-in-a-play-debate` Is ambition a crime? *(new)*. Task: Your class is discussing a character ruined by ambition, such as Macbeth or Julius Caesar. Say whether the character's ambition or the people around them caused the fall, support your view with two moments from the story, and answer a classmate who disagrees.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a scene of a play about ambition and hearing the motive behind the words; arguing who is to blame aloud.

### 4. War and Memory

**Words:** `words-of-war-and-conflict` Words of War and Conflict (after `context-clues`): belligerent, armistice, attrition, carnage, conscript, pacifist, insurgency, reprisal, devastation, veteran; `words-of-memory-and-witness` Words of Memory and Witness (after `context-clues`): lament, reminiscence, memorialize, atrocity, obliterate, oblivion, vestige, remembrance, requiem, cenotaph.

**Grammar:** `active-and-passive-for-emphasis` The Active and the Passive for Emphasis (after `shifts-in-voice`): Choosing the voice by what matters most: the active when the doer matters, the passive when the action or the receiver matters, or when the doer is unknown. Steps: Why This Voice; then The Voice That Keeps the Focus.

**Reading:** `tone-shifts-in-a-text` Shifts of Tone (after `the-cumulative-impact-of-word-choice`, `poetic-forms-and-meter`): Where and why the tone of a poem, story or speech turns, and the signal words and contrasts that mark the turn. Steps: From What to What; then The Signal of the Turn.

**Writing:** `writing-a-comparison-of-two-texts` Writing a Comparison of Two Texts (judged by a model; after `a-subject-in-two-mediums`, `writing-a-compare-and-contrast-essay`): Write an essay comparing how two texts present the same subject, explaining how differences of language and purpose create different views. Criteria: `g10-compare-claim` *(new)*, `g10-compare-language` *(new)*, `g10-compare-purpose` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `war-and-memory` War and Memory *(new)*, 8 passages: The Truce in the Trenches; A Battle of Attrition; The Red Flower; A Thousand Paper Cranes; The Soldier Without a Name; The Wall of Names; The Cathedral Left in Ruins; The Letter in the Drawer.

**Conversation:** `war-memorial-design-meeting` Designing a memorial *(new)*. Task: Your town is planning a memorial to the people of the town who died in a war, and the committee has asked students for ideas. Describe your design, explain what it should make visitors feel and remember, and answer a question about what it leaves out.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a veteran's or a survivor's account and noting where its tone shifts; describing a memorial aloud.

**Grammar:** `subordination-for-emphasis` Subordination for Emphasis (after `choosing-the-subordinating-conjunction`, `noun-adjective-and-adverb-clauses`): Putting the main idea in the main clause and the lesser idea in a dependent clause, so that the sentence stresses what the writer means to stress. **Reading:** `extended-metaphor` Extended Metaphor (after `similes-and-metaphors`, `symbolism`): A metaphor carried through several lines or a whole text, with each detail of one thing matched to a detail of the other. Steps: What Is Compared; then What the Detail Stands For.

**Writing:** `writing-a-descriptive-essay-on-a-place` Writing a Descriptive Essay on a Place (judged by a model; after `creating-atmosphere-with-sensory-details`, `writing-a-descriptive-paragraph`): Write a descriptive essay that brings a place to life through details of several senses, organized to build one dominant impression. **Reading unit:** `the-natural-world` The Natural World *(new)*, 8 passages: A Spring Without Birds; The Wolves That Moved Rivers; The Rabbits That Ate a Continent; The Forest That Makes Rain; The Black Blizzards; The Ice That Carved the Coast; An Ocean of Heather; The Ship Called Earth.

**Conversation:** `wetland-hearing` A hearing on the wetland *(new)*. Task: Your town council is deciding whether to drain a wetland at the edge of town to build a shopping center. **Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a nature documentary and noting its extended metaphor; speaking at a hearing on a place.

### 6. Media and Truth

**Words:** `words-of-journalism-and-ethics` Words of Journalism and Ethics (after `context-clues`): objectivity, slander, impartial, retract, allege, libel, sensationalism, whistleblower, credibility; `words-of-media-and-attention` Words of Media and Attention (after `context-clues`): influencer, platform, meme, clickbait, ubiquitous, curate, aggregate, notification, distraction, engagement.

**Reading:** `identifying-bias-in-the-news` Identifying Bias in the News (after `loaded-language`, `facts-interpretations-and-judgments`): How a news report can lean to one side without telling a lie: by its choice of words, of sources, of facts left out and of what goes in the headline. Steps: How the Report Leans; then The Neutral Version. `how-numbers-can-mislead` How Numbers Can Mislead (after `correlation-or-cause`, `reporting-numbers-from-a-graph`): The common ways true numbers give a false picture: a percentage without its base, an average that hides the spread, a cut-off axis and a sample too small or too chosen. Steps: The Trick in the Number; then The Question to Ask.

**Writing:** `keeping-an-objective-tone` Keeping an Objective Tone (after `formal-style`, `facts-interpretations-and-judgments`): Writing a report or an explanation in an objective tone: facts and attributed claims instead of personal feelings, loaded words and exaggeration. Steps: The Objective Sentence; then Fact, Attributed Claim or the Writer's Opinion. `writing-an-editorial` Writing an Editorial (judged by a model; after `writing-an-argument-with-sources`, `identifying-bias-in-the-news`): Write an editorial for a school or town newspaper that takes a clear position on a local issue, supports it with evidence, answers the other side and ends with a call to act. Criteria: `g10-editorial-position` *(new)*, `g10-editorial-evidence` *(new)*, `g10-editorial-other-side` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `media-and-truth` Media and Truth *(new)*, 8 passages: The Men on the Moon; Ten Days Inside; Remember the Maine; Follow the Money; The Poll That Asked the Wrong People; The Planes That Came Back; Built to Keep You Scrolling; The Clip That Went Everywhere.

**Conversation:** `newsroom-editorial-meeting` The editorial meeting *(new)*. Task: You are an editor of your school newspaper. In the weekly meeting, argue for the story that should lead the next issue, explain why readers need it, and answer the editor in chief, who worries that the story is unfair to someone.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to two news reports of one event and naming the bias of each; pitching a story at an editorial meeting.

### 8. Satire and Humor

**Words:** `words-of-satire-and-wit` Words of Satire and Wit (after `context-clues`): ridicule, witticism, lampoon, burlesque, irreverent, caustic, hyperbole, understatement, travesty, incongruity; `words-of-folly-and-vanity` Words of Folly and Vanity (after `context-clues`): folly, vanity, pompous, obsequious, sycophant, gullible, pretentious, complacent, charlatan, absurd.

**Reading:** `understanding-satire` Understanding Satire (after `irony`, `personification-and-hyperbole`): How satire uses humor to criticize: finding its target, recognizing its tools, and reading past the literal words to the point it makes. **Writing:** `writing-a-satire` Writing a Satire (judged by a model; after `understanding-satire`, `writing-an-argument-with-sources`): Write a short satire in a familiar form, such as an advertisement, an announcement or a news report, that exaggerates a real folly to make a serious point. Criteria: `g10-satire-target` *(new)*, `g10-satire-exaggeration` *(new)*, `g10-satire-voice` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `satire-and-humor` Satire and Humor *(new)*, 8 passages: A Proposal No One Could Accept; The War Over an Egg; The Clothes That Were Not There; The Spaghetti Harvest; The Jester and the Emperor; The Dictator on the Screen; The Pictures That Beat a Boss; The Ribbon at the End of the Road.

**Conversation:** `satire-writers-room` In the writers' room *(new)*. Task: You are writing a short satirical sketch for the school show with a partner. Propose a target, explain the exaggeration that will make the audience laugh and think, and answer your partner, who worries the sketch might hurt someone.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a satirical sketch and naming its target; planning a fair satire with a partner.

**Conversation:** `word-roots-challenge` The word roots challenge *(new)*. Task: You are a contestant in a school word challenge. When the host gives you an unfamiliar word, break it into parts, explain what each part means, work out the meaning of the word and use it in a sentence.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a talk on the roots of words and noting the parts it names; working out unfamiliar words aloud from their parts.

## What the Next Grade Builds On

`english-grade-11` builds on this year: the rhetorical analysis and the editorial grow into the argumentative essay on rights and laws and the synthesis of sources on science; reading for bias and misleading numbers goes on to logical fallacies, the evaluation of sources and historical documents; and grammar moves to absolute phrases, noun clauses and integrating quotations.
