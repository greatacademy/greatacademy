# English, Grade 6

> This is Great Academy's own outline. Its design is in the [English curriculum](../english-curriculum.md) §6.3 and the [development plan](../english-development-plan.md) §E8. [简体中文](english-grade-6.zh-CN.md).

- **Course:** `english-grade-6`
- **Level:** Grade 6, for learners whose first language is English or who read English as well as a native speaker of their age
- **Prerequisite(s):** `english-grade-5`, or reading and writing reports, opinion pieces and stories at the level of grade 5
- **Language of explanation:** English
- **Size:** 10 units · 108 topics (24 existing, 84 new: 36 of vocabulary, 16 of grammar and mechanics, 15 of reading, 10 of writing checked by rules, 7 of writing judged by a model) · about 1,560 XP · 9 reading units of 74 passages (3 existing, 6 new) · 10 conversations (3 existing, 7 new)

## Aim

By the end of grade 6 a learner reads the myths, poems, memoirs, histories and science texts of the grade on their own. In stories they follow plot and conflict, name a theme and the archetypes a tale is built on, say what a narrator knows and how a setting shapes what happens; in poems they tell figurative from literal language and hear its sound and tone, and they compare a poem and a story on one theme. In texts of facts they trace how an idea is introduced and developed, say what a sentence does in the whole, work out technical words, use the features of a science text, follow causes and effects in history, and tell supported claims from unsupported ones and the author's point of view. They use who and whom, who, which and that, intensive pronouns, clear pronouns that keep their number, verbs that agree across phrases and with either, or and neither, nor, the irregular past participles, good and well; they punctuate nonessential elements and colons, write possessives, numbers and titles, and spell words with doubled consonants and prefixes. They paraphrase a source, link ideas with transitions, define terms, write precise and consistent prose, show character through action, open with a hook and end a narrative; and they write an explanation of history, an explanation of a process, a compare-and-contrast essay, a memoir scene, a letter to a leader, a sports column and the history of a word. They know the words of ten themes: heroes and fate, light, sound and movement, Roman law and building, the first cities, the restless Earth, living cells, memory and friendship, food and farming, sport and fair play, and where words come from.

## How to Read This Outline

Every unit is a theme and teaches four things together: the words of the theme, grammar and mechanics, reading skills, and writing. It ends with a reading unit on the theme and a conversation, except the last, which studies words and has no reading unit. Topics marked *existing* are in the graph already, in the band course `english-grades-6-8`, and keep their ids and their own prerequisites; each new topic lists its prerequisites, which may lie in an earlier unit or an earlier grade. A new topic of words lists the words it teaches, none of which another batch of the native courses teaches; any other new topic says what it teaches and its knowledge points as steps. A judged writing topic names its task and the criteria of its rubric, marking the criteria that are new.

Strands: of the 108 topics, 41 teach words (38%), 21 grammar and mechanics (19%), 22 reading (20%), 14 writing checked by rules (13%) and 10 writing judged by a model (9%).

Depth: each new grammar, reading or rule-checked writing knowledge point has 30 to 48 items built from hand-written cores by templates; each word of a vocabulary batch appears in three sentences, so each vocabulary knowledge point has 30 items; each judged writing knowledge point has 12 tasks with a model; reading units have 8 to 10 passages of 200 to 400 words, with three questions each. A batch of ten words earns about 15 XP, a word-study, grammar, reading or rule-checked writing topic about 14 and a judged writing topic about 22, so the lessons of the grade come to about 1,560 XP.

Listening, speaking and phonics wait for audio and recording: until then a unit lists its conversation but does not offer it, and each unit says what it will gain.

The band course `english-grades-6-8` retires once `english-grade-6`, `english-grade-7` and `english-grade-8` hold all of its topics. Its 55 topics are divided among the three grades by its units, each in exactly one, with its id unchanged: 24 in grade 6 (myths, poetry and Rome), 16 in grade 7 (argument and oceans) and 15 in grade 8 (turning points, machines and exploration). Its reading units and conversations go with their units.

**Reading:** `how-a-sentence-fits-the-whole` How a Sentence Fits the Whole (after `text-structures`): Each sentence or paragraph does a job in a text: it states the central idea, gives an example or evidence, defines a term, moves to a new point or sums up. ### 8. Food for Everyone

**Words:** `words-of-nutrition` Words of Nutrition (after `context-clues`): protein, vitamin, calorie, diet, nutrition, carbohydrate, fiber, nutritious, sodium, wholesome; `words-of-modern-farming` Words of Modern Farming (after `context-clues`): pesticide, fertilizer, yield, livestock, organic, cultivate, graze, fallow, erosion, pasture; `words-of-hunger-and-plenty` Words of Hunger and Plenty (after `context-clues`): shortage, famine, distribute, feast, abundance, starve, hoard, plentiful, malnutrition, scarcity. `prefixes-of-place-and-direction` Prefixes of Place and Direction (after `common-prefixes`): *sub-* (under), *inter-* (between), *trans-* (across), *ex-* (out), *intra-* (within), *circum-* (around), *super-* (above), *peri-* (around, near), *pro-* (forward), *retro-* (back). Steps: choosing the direction a prefix means; then choosing the word that fits a sentence.

**Grammar:** `colons-before-lists-and-explanations` Colons Before Lists and Explanations (after `commas-in-a-series`): A colon follows a complete sentence and introduces a list or an explanation (*Plants need three things: light, water and air*); it does not follow *such as*, *include* or a verb. **Reading:** `tracing-an-argument` Tracing an Argument (after `authors-purpose`, `matching-points-and-evidence`): An argument has a claim, reasons that back it and evidence for each reason; tracing it means finding each part and seeing how they connect. Steps: choosing the claim of an argument; then matching a piece of evidence to the reason it supports. `inferring-an-authors-point-of-view` The Author's Point of View (after `authors-purpose`): An author's point of view shows in what they choose to include, the words they use and how they describe other views. Steps: choosing the author's point of view from their words; then choosing the sentence that shows how they treat another view.

**Writing:** `choosing-relevant-reasons` Choosing Relevant Reasons (after `ordering-reasons-logically`): A reason is relevant when it shows why the claim is true; one that is true but about something else, or only a feeling, weakens the piece. Steps: telling a relevant reason from an irrelevant one; then choosing the strongest of three reasons. `hooks-for-an-opening` Hooks for an Opening (after `previewing-the-topic`): An opening catches the reader with a question, a surprising fact, a short anecdote or a quotation that fits the topic. Steps: naming the kind of hook an opening uses; then choosing the hook that fits a piece. `writing-a-letter-to-a-leader` Writing a Letter to a Leader (judged by a model; after `writing-an-opinion-piece`, `choosing-relevant-reasons`): A letter to a mayor, principal or other leader that asks for one change about food or farming, gives relevant reasons with facts, and keeps a respectful tone. Criteria: `g6-states-request` *(new)*, `g6-relevant-reasons` *(new)*, `g6-respectful-tone` *(new)*, `w4-concludes`.

**Reading unit:** `feeding-the-world` *(new)*, 8 passages: What Is in Your Lunch?; The Green Revolution; Farms in the City; Bees and Our Food; Wasted Food; A Famine Remembered; The Seed Vault; Should Schools Grow Their Own Food?

**Conversation:** `school-lunch-committee` The School Lunch Committee *(new)*. The tutor chairs a committee on the school's lunches. Task: propose one change to the menu, give reasons and answer an objection about cost.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a short talk on food waste and tracing its argument; giving a one-minute speech for a change to the lunch menu.

### 9. Sport and Fair Play

**Words:** `words-of-competition` Words of Competition (after `context-clues`): opponent, championship, referee, spectator, compete, victory, defeat, league, contender, rally; `words-of-rules-and-fair-play` Words of Rules and Fair Play (after `context-clues`): penalty, foul, violate, regulation, eligible, disqualify, cheat, sportsmanship, forfeit, suspend; `words-of-training` Words of Training (after `context-clues`): stamina, agility, strain, coordination, drill, sprain, hydrate, sprint, conditioning, regimen.

**Grammar:** `good-and-well-bad-and-badly` Good and Well, Bad and Badly (after `adjectives-and-adverbs`): *Good* and *bad* are adjectives, used after linking verbs (*feel bad*, *looks good*); *well* and *badly* are adverbs for actions (*played well*), and *well* is also an adjective for health. Steps: choosing the adjective or the adverb by the verb; then choosing between *feel well* and *feel good* by meaning. `capitals-in-titles-and-headings` Capitals in Titles and Headings (after `titles-in-italics-and-quotation-marks`): In a title the first, last and every important word take capitals; short articles, conjunctions and prepositions inside it do not. Steps: choosing whether a word of a title takes a capital; then choosing the title written correctly. `spelling-words-with-prefixes` Spelling Words with Prefixes (after `common-prefixes`): A prefix is added without changing the spelling of the word, so letters can double (*misspell*, *unnatural*, *illegal*); *in-* becomes *im-* before *b*, *m* and *p*, *il-* before *l* and *ir-* before *r*. Steps: choosing the form of *in-* a word takes; then choosing the correct spelling of a word with a prefix.

**Reading:** `reading-rules-and-instructions` Reading Rules and Instructions (after `sequence-in-texts`): Rules say what must, may and must not be done, often with exceptions and conditions. Steps: choosing what a rule requires; then applying a rule and its exception to a case.

**Writing:** `writing-a-sports-column` Writing a Sports Column (judged by a model; after `writing-a-letter-to-a-leader`): A column for the school paper that takes a position on a question of fair play, such as whether every player should get equal time, supports it with examples and answers the other side. Criteria: `g6-takes-a-position` *(new)*, `g6-supports-with-examples` *(new)*, `g6-answers-other-side` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `fair-play` *(new)*, 8 passages: The First Marathon; Why We Have Referees; A Race Run Fairly; Practice Makes Progress; The Rules of the Game; Winning and Losing Well; Girls on the Field; Should Video Replay Decide?

**Conversation:** `referee-call-dispute` A Call at the Game *(new)*. The tutor is a youth league referee after a disputed call. Task: politely ask about the call, listen to the rule, and explain what your team will do next time.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a sports commentary and noting the facts and the opinions; giving a spoken argument about a rule.

### 10. Where Words Come From

**Words:** `words-named-after-people` Words Named After People (after `context-clues`): sandwich, boycott, cardigan, saxophone, diesel, braille, leotard, silhouette, maverick, pasteurize; `words-about-language` Words About Language (after `context-clues`): dialect, accent, slang, jargon, idiom, fluent, bilingual, pronunciation, vocabulary, translate; `words-of-time-and-change` Words of Time and Change (after `context-clues`): obsolete, evolve, permanent, temporary, epoch, prehistoric, contemporary, millennium, chronological, antique. `words-from-other-languages` Words from Other Languages (after `latin-roots-3`): English borrows words from many languages: *kindergarten* from German, *ballet* from French, *piano* from Italian, *tsunami* from Japanese, *ketchup* from Chinese, *safari* from Swahili. Steps: choosing the language a borrowed word came from by its clue; then choosing the borrowed word that fits a sentence.

**Reading:** `word-choice-and-meaning` Word Choice and Meaning (after `connotation-and-precise-words`): An author's choice of one word over another shapes meaning and tone: *crept* and *strode*, *stubborn* and *determined*, *old* and *ancient*. Steps: choosing what a word choice suggests about a person or a scene; then choosing the word that gives a passage the tone it aims for.

**Writing:** `writing-a-word-history` Writing the History of a Word (judged by a model; after `writing-a-history-explanation`, `words-from-other-languages`): From notes on a word's origin, a paragraph that tells where the word came from, how its meaning changed and why, and what it means now. Criteria: `g6-word-origin` *(new)*, `g6-explains-change` *(new)*, `w4-concludes`.

**Conversation:** `word-origins-quiz-show` A Word Origins Quiz Show *(new)*. The tutor hosts a quiz show on the stories of words. Task: guess where three words came from, explain your reasons, and then tell the host the story of one word you know.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to the stories of three words and noting their origins; saying the same word in two accents and describing the difference.

## What the Next Grade Builds On

`english-grade-7` builds on this year: tracing an argument grows into writing one with a thesis, evidence paragraphs and a counterclaim; supported claims lead to evidence and reasoning; the explanation of a process and the compare-and-contrast essay lead to the objective summary and the informative essay; grammar moves to active and passive voice, verb moods and parallel structure; and the themes of the restless Earth and living cells go on to the oceans and their ecosystems.
