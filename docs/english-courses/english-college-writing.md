# College Writing

> This is Great Academy's own outline. Its design is in the [English curriculum](../english-curriculum.md) §11. [简体中文](english-college-writing.zh-CN.md).

- **Course:** `english-college-writing`
- **Level:** First year of university, and adults who write for study or work
- **Prerequisite(s):** [English, Grade 12](english-grade-12.md) (`buildsOn: ["english-grade-12"]`)
- **Language of explanation:** English
- **Size:** 8 units · 38 topics, all new (8 of vocabulary, 9 of grammar, style and mechanics, 8 of reading, 13 of writing judged by a model) · 8 reading units of 8 passages each · 8 conversations

## Aim

By the end of the course a learner reads academic articles, data and workplace documents critically: they find the question a text answers, its claim, its evidence and its place among other work. They do research honestly: they form a question, find and weigh scholarly sources, quote, paraphrase and cite them in MLA and APA style. They write a research paper with a literature review (W6), and the documents of working life: emails, memos, cover letters, executive summaries and proposals, in clear, concise and cohesive prose. Every piece of writing is judged by a model against published criteria; there is no human review.

## How to Read This Outline

Every unit is a theme and teaches four things together: words, sentences and style, a kind of reading, and writing. It ends with a reading unit on the theme and a conversation. Each topic lists its prerequisites; topics of grades 3 to 12 count as known.

Depth: each rule-checked knowledge point has at least 30 items built from hand-written cores by templates; each word of a vocabulary batch appears in three sentences; each judged writing knowledge point has at least 12 tasks with a model, and the research paper is split into three knowledge points that can be judged one at a time (the introduction, with its context, gap and thesis; the discussion; the whole paper with its references), the literature review being a topic of its own. Each reading unit has at least 8 passages of 500 to 900 words with three questions each.

Listening and speaking wait for audio and recording: until then a unit lists its conversation but does not offer it, and each unit says what it will gain.

## Units

### 1. Reading Academic Prose

**Words:** `words-of-academic-discourse` Words of Academic Discourse (after `context-clues`): paradigm, framework, methodology, discourse, implication, premise, critique, assumption, phenomenon, contend.

**Style:** `hedging-and-qualification` Hedging and Qualification (after `modal-verbs`, `noun-clauses`): *may*, *suggests*, *in most cases*, and claims as strong as their evidence.

**Reading:** `reading-academic-arguments` Reading Academic Arguments (after `evaluating-sources`, `rhetorical-situation`): the question, the claim, and how an article answers earlier work.

**Writing:** `writing-a-critical-summary` Writing a Critical Summary (judged by a model; after `writing-an-objective-summary`, `reading-academic-arguments`): an article's argument in a paragraph, then one strength and one limitation.

**Reading unit:** `academic-prose`, 8 original passages in the manner of journal articles: a study of memory; the minimum wage; a debate among historians; how children learn grammar; birds in cities; teenagers and social media; free will; a language that is dying.

**Conversation:** `professor-office-hours`. The tutor is a professor in office hours. Task: explain what you found hard in an assigned article and ask a focused question about it.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a lecture and taking notes of its argument; asking questions in office hours aloud.

### 2. Questions and Sources

**Words:** `words-of-research` Words of Research (after `context-clues`): bibliography, abstract, database, scholarly, journal, archive, interdisciplinary, credentials, retrieval, annotation.

**Style:** `verbs-for-citing-sources` Verbs for Citing Sources (after `integrating-quotations`): *argues*, *claims*, *notes*, *concedes*, *refutes*, and what each says about the source.

**Reading:** `finding-scholarly-sources` Finding Scholarly Sources (after `evaluating-sources`): peer review, abstracts, search terms, and telling a scholarly source from a popular one.

**Writing:** `forming-a-research-question` Forming a Research Question (judged by a model; after `reading-academic-arguments`): a question narrow enough to answer and open enough to argue. `writing-an-annotated-bibliography` Writing an Annotated Bibliography (judged by a model; after `finding-scholarly-sources`, `writing-a-critical-summary`).

**Reading unit:** `research-in-practice`, 8 original essays on how invented researchers work: Choosing a Question; A Search That Failed; An Archive in a Basement; Interviews in a Village; The Lab Notebook; An Experiment That Surprised; A Collaboration Across Fields; A Result That Would Not Replicate.

**Conversation:** `librarian-research-consultation`. The tutor is a research librarian. Task: describe your topic, narrow it to a question, and leave with two search strategies.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to researchers describe their work and noting their questions; explaining a research plan aloud.

### 3. Using Sources Honestly

**Words:** `words-of-evidence-and-inference` Words of Evidence and Inference (after `context-clues`): corroborate, substantiate, attribute, paraphrase, verbatim, excerpt, discrepancy, extrapolate, verify, provenance.

**Mechanics:** `quoting-and-paraphrasing` Quoting and Paraphrasing (after `integrating-quotations`): when to quote, when to paraphrase, and what counts as plagiarism. `citing-in-mla-and-apa` Citing in MLA and APA (after `quoting-and-paraphrasing`): in-text citations and reference entries for books, articles and web pages.

**Reading:** `tracing-claims-to-sources` Tracing Claims to Sources (after `evaluating-sources`): following a claim back to its origin and noticing where it changed.

**Writing:** `writing-a-paraphrase` Writing a Paraphrase (judged by a model; after `quoting-and-paraphrasing`): a passage restated faithfully in new words and structure, with its citation.

**Reading unit:** `the-ethics-of-knowledge`, 8 original essays, the cases invented: The Fraud That Fooled a Field; How a Paper Is Retracted; How Peer Review Works; A Short History of the Footnote; An Encyclopedia Anyone Can Edit; A Statistic Without a Source; Credit Among Co-Authors; Copying and Credit.

**Conversation:** `writing-center-citation-help`. The tutor is a writing center tutor. Task: show where you used a source in a draft, and work out together how to quote or paraphrase and cite it correctly.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a podcast and finding the sources it relies on; explaining a citation decision aloud.

### 4. The Research Paper

**Words:** `words-of-scholarly-argument` Words of Scholarly Argument (after `context-clues`): albeit, notwithstanding, thereby, whereas, insofar, hence, conversely, nonetheless, moreover, furthermore.

**Style:** `cohesion-old-before-new` Cohesion: Old Before New (after `choosing-transitions`, `word-order-for-emphasis`): beginning sentences with what the reader knows and ending with what is new.

**Reading:** `reading-a-research-paper` Reading a Research Paper (after `reading-academic-arguments`): the abstract, introduction, methods, results and discussion, and what to read first.

**Writing:** `writing-a-literature-review` Writing a Literature Review (judged by a model; after `writing-an-annotated-bibliography`, `synthesizing-sources`). `writing-a-research-paper` Writing a Research Paper (judged by a model, W6; after `writing-a-literature-review`, `citing-in-mla-and-apa`, `writing-an-argumentative-essay`).

**Reading unit:** `inside-a-research-paper`, 8 passages that follow one invented study, of green space near schools and children's attention, from start to finish: The Question; The Literature; The Method; The Data; The Results; The Discussion; The Limitations; The Response of Other Researchers.

**Conversation:** `peer-review-workshop`. The tutor is a classmate whose draft you reviewed. Task: give feedback on their thesis and evidence, and answer their questions about your comments.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a conference talk and summarizing its findings; presenting a paper's argument in three minutes.

### 5. Style and Clarity

**Words:** `words-often-misused` Words Often Misused (after `connotation-and-precise-words`): comprise, disinterested, enormity, literally, infer, ironic, decimate, fortuitous, peruse, bemused.

**Style:** `clear-sentences-characters-and-actions` Clear Sentences: Characters and Actions (after `cutting-wordiness`): main characters as subjects, actions as verbs, and undoing nominalizations.

**Reading:** `analyzing-prose-style` Analyzing Prose Style (after `rhetorical-devices`): diction, syntax and rhythm, and how they shape a reader's experience.

**Writing:** `revising-for-clarity` Revising for Clarity (judged by a model; after `clear-sentences-characters-and-actions`, `revising-a-draft`): a dense paragraph rewritten to be clear without losing its meaning.

**Reading unit:** `essays-on-writing`, 8 original essays on writing: Why Jargon Spreads; Plain Language in Law; The Long Sentence and the Short; Writing for a Screen; The Passive Voice Defended; How Editors Read; Writing in a Second Language; Revision as Rethinking.

**Conversation:** `editor-style-review`. The tutor is an editor. Task: defend or revise three choices in your paragraph after the editor questions them.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a passage read aloud and hearing where it is unclear; reading your own revision aloud.

### 6. Numbers in Arguments

**Words:** `words-of-statistics` Words of Statistics (after `context-clues`): median, percentile, sample, margin, proportion, trend, outlier, distribution, probability, significant.

**Mechanics:** `numbers-in-writing` Numbers in Writing (after `capitalizing-names-and-titles`): numerals or words, percentages, units, dates and ranges.

**Reading:** `reading-charts-and-statistics` Reading Charts and Statistics (after `evaluating-sources`, `logical-fallacies`): what a chart shows and hides, correlation and cause, and misleading scales.

**Writing:** `writing-about-data` Writing About Data (judged by a model; after `reading-charts-and-statistics`, `numbers-in-writing`, `writing-an-explanatory-essay`): a paragraph that reports a table or chart accurately and draws only the conclusion it supports.

**Reading unit:** `numbers-in-the-news`, 8 original articles, each with an invented table or figure described in words: A Poll Before an Election; Crime Rates and What They Count; The Price of a Home; Screen Time and Sleep; Test Scores and Rankings; Air on a Bad Day; A Medical Risk; The Best Shooter in the League.

**Conversation:** `presenting-findings-to-a-panel`. The tutor chairs a panel. Task: present one finding from a small study and answer a question about what the data cannot show.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a news story with numbers and checking its claims; presenting a chart aloud.

### 7. Writing at Work

**Words:** `words-of-the-workplace` Words of the Workplace (after `context-clues`): deadline, stakeholder, agenda, deliverable, budget, colleague, feedback, collaborate, delegate, prioritize.

**Style:** `tone-and-register-at-work` Tone and Register at Work (after `connotation-and-precise-words`, `verb-moods`): polite requests, direct refusals and the level of formality a reader expects.

**Reading:** `reading-workplace-documents` Reading Workplace Documents (after `text-features`): job postings, policies, minutes and instructions, and what they require of the reader.

**Writing:** `writing-a-professional-email` Writing a Professional Email (judged by a model; after `tone-and-register-at-work`). `writing-a-memo` Writing a Memo (judged by a model; after `writing-a-professional-email`). `writing-a-cover-letter` Writing a Cover Letter (judged by a model; after `writing-a-professional-email`, `reading-workplace-documents`).

**Reading unit:** `writing-at-work`, 8 original model documents, each followed by a commentary: A Request to a Manager; Saying No Well; A Meeting Summary; A Memo on a New Policy; Reading a Job Posting; A Cover Letter; A Complaint and Its Reply; Instructions for a New Hire.

**Conversation:** `job-interview-practice`. The tutor is an interviewer for a job you choose. Task: answer three questions about your experience with specific examples, and ask one question about the job.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a voicemail and writing the email it calls for; answering interview questions aloud.

### 8. Proposals and Reports

**Words:** `words-of-planning-and-proposals` Words of Planning and Proposals (after `context-clues`): feasible, objective, rationale, milestone, scope, allocate, evaluate, sustainable, mitigate, implement.

**Style:** `parallel-lists-and-headings` Parallel Lists and Headings (after `parallel-structure`).

**Reading:** `reading-requests-for-proposals` Reading Requests for Proposals (after `reading-workplace-documents`): what a funder asks for, how it will judge, and what is required.

**Writing:** `writing-an-executive-summary` Writing an Executive Summary (judged by a model; after `writing-a-critical-summary`, `writing-a-memo`). `writing-a-research-proposal` Writing a Research Proposal (judged by a model; after `forming-a-research-question`, `writing-an-executive-summary`).

**Reading unit:** `plans-that-worked`, 8 original accounts of proposals and what came of them: A Grant for a Community Garden; A School Recycling Plan; A Pitch for a Small Business; The City That Built Bicycle Lanes; A Research Grant; A Library Open Late; A Clinic in a Village; A Plan That Failed.

**Conversation:** `client-project-meeting`. The tutor is a client who has asked for a proposal. Task: present your plan's aim, schedule and cost, and agree on one change the client asks for.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a client's brief and noting its requirements; pitching a proposal aloud.
