# English, Grade 5

> This is Great Academy's own outline. Its design is in the [English curriculum](../english-curriculum.md) §6.3 and the [development plan](../english-development-plan.md) §E8. [简体中文](english-grade-5.zh-CN.md).

- **Course:** `english-grade-5`
- **Level:** Grade 5, for learners whose first language is English or who read English as well as a native speaker of their age
- **Prerequisite(s):** `english-grade-4`, or reading and writing reports and stories at the level of grade 4
- **Language of explanation:** English
- **Size:** 10 units · 114 topics (12 existing, 102 new: 40 of vocabulary, 19 of grammar and mechanics, 20 of reading, 14 of writing checked by rules, 9 of writing judged by a model) · about 1,670 XP · 10 reading units of 82 passages (2 existing, 8 new) · 10 conversations (2 existing, 8 new)

## Aim

By the end of grade 5 a learner reads the stories, poems, plays and informational texts of the grade on their own and quotes them accurately. They find two or more main ideas in a text, compare the structures of two texts, tell fact from opinion and the author's purpose, weigh several accounts of one event and gather answers from several sources; in stories they compare characters, follow how chapters build a story and how a mystery plants its clues, and tell who speaks in a poem. They use the perfect tenses, correlative conjunctions, indefinite pronouns and interjections, shift tense only for a reason, keep pronoun person steady, and use commas after introductions, in series and before tag questions, italics and quotation marks for titles, and hyphens. They write answers backed by evidence from a text, a write-up of an experiment, a report of three paragraphs, an opinion piece with a paragraph for each reason, a piece about two texts, a news article, a mystery, a personal narrative and a diary from history, and proofread their own work. They know the words of ten themes: water, matter, the world of cells and microbes, space, ancient Greece, the Maya, Aztec and Inca, engineering, mysteries, survival and life long ago, with the Greek and Latin roots of science.

## How to Read This Outline

Every unit is a theme and teaches four things together: the words of the theme, grammar and mechanics, reading skills, and writing. It ends with a reading unit on the theme and a conversation. Topics marked *existing* are in the graph already, in the band course `english-grades-3-5`, and keep their ids and their own prerequisites; each new topic lists its prerequisites, which may lie in an earlier unit or an earlier grade. A new topic of words lists the words it teaches, none of which another batch of the native courses teaches; any other new topic says what it teaches and its knowledge points as steps. A judged writing topic names its task and the criteria of its rubric, marking the criteria that are new.

Strands: of the 114 topics, 44 teach words (39%), 23 grammar and mechanics (20%), 23 reading (20%), 14 writing checked by rules (12%) and 10 writing judged by a model (9%).

Depth: each new grammar, reading or rule-checked writing knowledge point has 30 to 48 items built from hand-written cores by templates; each word of a vocabulary batch appears in three sentences, so each vocabulary knowledge point has 30 items; each judged writing knowledge point has 12 tasks with a model; reading units have 8 to 10 passages of 140 to 260 words, with three questions each. A batch of ten words earns about 15 XP, a word-study, grammar, reading or rule-checked writing topic about 14 and a judged writing topic about 22, so the lessons of the grade come to about 1,670 XP.

Listening, speaking and phonics wait for audio and recording: until then a unit lists its conversation but does not offer it, and each unit says what it will gain.

The band course `english-grades-3-5` retires once `english-grade-3`, `english-grade-4` and `english-grade-5` hold all of its topics. Its 78 topics are divided among the three grades, each in exactly one, with its id unchanged: 45 in grade 3, 21 in grade 4 and 12 in grade 5.

**Writing:** `asking-a-research-question` Asking a Research Question (after `skimming-and-scanning`): A good research question is narrow enough to answer from a few sources and cannot be answered with yes or no. **Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a short radio report on a drought and noting its main idea; explaining the water cycle aloud in order.

Steps: choosing which detail is a real clue to the culprit; then choosing why another detail misleads. `drawing-conclusions` Drawing Conclusions (after `making-inferences`, `supporting-details`): A conclusion puts several clues together into one judgment that none of them states alone. Steps: choosing the conclusion that all the clues support; then choosing the clue that rules out another conclusion. `building-suspense` How Writers Build Suspense (after `how-setting-shapes-a-story`, `making-predictions`): Writers build suspense with a dark or closed setting, short sentences, questions left open and endings of chapters that stop at a moment of danger. Steps: choosing the sentence that builds suspense; then choosing how a writer builds it in a passage. `comparing-stories-of-one-genre` Comparing Stories of One Genre (after `same-pattern-different-cultures`): Stories of one genre share features (a mystery has a puzzle, a detective, suspects and a solution) but treat them differently. Steps: choosing the feature of a genre a story shows; then choosing how two stories of the genre differ in it.

**Writing:** `planning-a-story-arc` Planning a Story Arc (after `setting-problem-and-solution`, `ending-a-story`): A story arc goes from a beginning that sets up the problem, through events that raise the stakes, to a turning point and an ending. Steps: placing events of a plan on the arc; then choosing the event that should be the turning point. `writing-a-mystery` Writing a Mystery (judged by a model; after `writing-a-narrative-paragraph`, `planning-a-story-arc`, `clues-and-red-herrings`): A short mystery of several paragraphs: a puzzle that is set up early, clues the reader can follow, one red herring, a solution that the clues explain and scenes that show rather than tell. Criteria: `g5-mystery-puzzle` *(new)*, `g5-mystery-clues` *(new)*, `g5-mystery-solution` *(new)*, `g35-narrative-shows`.

### 9. Stories of Survival

**Words:** `words-of-wilderness-survival` Words of Wilderness Survival (after `context-clues`): wilderness, stranded, ration, flint, kindling, canteen, raft, blister, dehydrate, trek; `words-of-ships-and-the-sea` Words of Ships and the Sea (after `context-clues`): shipwreck, castaway, lifeboat, mast, anchor, deck, hull, oar, rigging, starboard; `words-for-courage-and-fear` Words for Courage and Fear (after `context-clues`): courage, dread, desperate, bold, despair, hesitate, tremble, persist, grit, cower; `words-for-choices-and-consequences` Words for Choices and Consequences (after `context-clues`): consequence, decision, reckless, regret, sacrifice, dilemma, judgment, urgent, crucial, gamble.

**Grammar:** `interjections` Interjections (after `four-kinds-of-sentences`, `choosing-punctuation-for-effect`): An interjection shows feeling and stands apart from the sentence, with an exclamation mark when strong (*Help!*) and a comma when mild (*Oh, the fire is out*). Steps: finding the interjection; then choosing its punctuation. `however-and-therefore` However and Therefore (after `choosing-transitions`, `run-ons-and-comma-splices`): Words such as *however*, *therefore* and *meanwhile* do not join two sentences with a comma alone: they start a new sentence or follow a semicolon, with a comma after them. Steps: telling a correct sentence from a comma splice; then fixing the splice. `preposition-or-adverb` Preposition or Adverb (after `prepositional-phrases`, `adjectives-and-adverbs`): A word such as *up*, *down*, *inside* or *across* is a preposition when it has an object (*across the river*) and an adverb when it stands alone (*She swam across*). Steps: telling which it is in a sentence; then finding the object of a preposition.

**Reading:** `comparing-two-characters` Comparing Two Characters (after `what-characters-say-and-think`, `compare-and-contrast-in-texts`): Two characters in one story can face the same problem in different ways. Steps: choosing how two characters are alike; then choosing how they differ and the detail that shows it. `how-characters-face-challenges` How Characters Face Challenges (after `why-characters-act`, `character-change`): How a character responds to a challenge shows who they are and often changes them. Steps: choosing how a character responds to a challenge; then choosing what the response shows about them. `how-chapters-build-a-story` How Chapters Build a Story (after `parts-of-stories-and-poems`, `summarizing-a-story`): Each chapter of a longer story does a job: it introduces, complicates, turns or resolves. Steps: choosing the job of a chapter from its summary; then putting chapter summaries in order.

**Writing:** `pacing-a-story` Pacing a Story (after `planning-a-story-arc`, `showing-not-telling`): A writer slows down at the moments that matter, with detail and dialogue, and speeds up the rest with a summary (*Three days passed*). Steps: choosing which moment of a story to slow down; then choosing the sentence that speeds past a dull stretch. `writing-a-personal-narrative` Writing a Personal Narrative (judged by a model; after `writing-a-narrative-paragraph`, `pacing-a-story`): A true story of several paragraphs about a time the writer faced a challenge: it sets the situation, slows down at the key moment, shows the writer's feelings and ends with what they learned. Criteria: `g35-narrative-situation`, `g5-personal-pacing` *(new)*, `g5-personal-feelings` *(new)*, `g5-personal-reflection` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `stories-of-survival` Stories of Survival *(new)*, 8 passages: Lost on the Ridge; The Flood Night; Three Days on the Island; Whiteout; The Broken Canoe; The Desert Detour; Adrift; A Night in the Cave.

**Conversation:** `wilderness-ranger-safety-briefing` A ranger's safety briefing *(new)*. The tutor is a ranger before a long hike. Task: find out what to pack, what to do if you get lost and how to signal for help, and repeat the plan back.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a survival story told aloud and noting where the pace slows; telling a true story of a challenge aloud.

**Grammar:** `titles-in-italics-and-quotation-marks` Titles in Italics and Quotation Marks (after `capitalizing-names-and-titles`, `quotation-marks-for-quotations`): Titles of whole works (books, newspapers, films) are in italics or underlined; titles of parts (poems, chapters, articles, songs) are in quotation marks. Steps: telling a whole work from a part; then choosing how its title is marked. `shifts-in-pronoun-person` Shifts in Pronoun Person (after `pronoun-antecedent-agreement`): A piece keeps one person: it does not slide from *we* or *one* to *you* without reason (*When we left, you could see the smoke* → *we could see*). Steps: finding the shift; then choosing the pronoun that keeps the person steady. `commonly-confused-words-2` More Commonly Confused Words (after `commonly-confused-words`, `contractions`): *Whose* and *who's*, *lose* and *loose*, *accept* and *except*, *quiet* and *quite*, *desert* and *dessert*, *affect* and *effect*. Steps: choosing the word that fits a sentence; then finding and fixing the wrong one.

**Reading:** `reading-diaries-and-letters` Reading Diaries and Letters (after `reading-a-letter`, `firsthand-and-secondhand-accounts`): A diary or a letter from the past is a primary record: it shows what one person saw and felt, and its details tell how life was then. Steps: choosing what a detail of an old letter tells about life at the time; then choosing what the writer felt and how the reader knows. `the-speaker-of-a-poem` The Speaker of a Poem (after `what-a-poem-is-about`, `point-of-view`): The voice of a poem is its speaker, who may not be the poet: a child, an old sailor, a tree. Steps: choosing who the speaker of a poem is; then choosing how the speaker's view shapes what the poem describes.

**Writing:** `proofreading` Proofreading (after `commas-in-a-series`, `commonly-confused-words`): Before a piece is finished, the writer checks it for one kind of error at a time: capitals, end marks, commas, spelling, confused words. Steps: finding the error of a given kind in a short paragraph; then finding every error in it. `writing-a-diary-from-history` Writing a Diary from History (judged by a model; after `reading-diaries-and-letters`, `writing-a-narrative-paragraph`): Two or three diary entries by an invented person of a past time the learner has read about: a first-person voice, details true to the period, the writer's feelings and events in order by date. Criteria: `g5-diary-voice` *(new)*, `g5-diary-period` *(new)*, `g5-diary-feelings` *(new)*, `g5-diary-order` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `letters-and-diaries-from-the-past` Letters and Diaries from the Past *(new)*, 8 passages: A Letter from the Canal Boat; The Lighthouse Keeper's Log; Dear Mama, from the Gold Fields; A Diary of the Great Flood; Letters Across the Ocean; A Schoolgirl's Diary, 1910; A Telegram Home; Notes from a Mountain Village.

**Conversation:** `time-capsule-planning` Planning a time capsule *(new)*. The tutor is a classmate planning a time capsule to open in fifty years. Task: agree on three things to put in it and what the letter inside should say about life today.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to an old letter read aloud and noting what it tells about life then; reading a diary entry aloud in character.

## What the Next Grade Builds On

`english-grades-6-8` builds on this year: the opinion piece grows into an argument with a claim, a counterclaim and cited evidence, and the report into an explanatory essay; grammar moves to relative clauses, simple, compound and complex sentences, pronoun case and active and passive voice; reading turns to theme, plot and conflict, point of view in narration, tone, sound devices, personification and hyperbole, and citing textual evidence; and the themes of Greece, the Americas and the sciences go on to the history and science of the middle grades.
