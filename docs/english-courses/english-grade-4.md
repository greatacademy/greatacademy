# English, Grade 4

> This is Great Academy's own outline. Its design is in the [English curriculum](../english-curriculum.md) §6.3 and the [development plan](../english-development-plan.md) §E8. [简体中文](english-grade-4.zh-CN.md).

- **Course:** `english-grade-4`
- **Level:** Grade 4, for learners whose first language is English or who read English as well as a native speaker of their age
- **Prerequisite(s):** `english-grade-3`, or reading and writing paragraphs at the level of grade 3
- **Language of explanation:** English
- **Size:** 10 units · 114 topics (21 existing, 93 new: 38 of vocabulary, 15 of grammar and mechanics, 20 of reading, 14 of writing checked by rules, 6 of writing judged by a model) · about 1,640 XP · 10 reading units of 84 passages (2 existing, 8 new) · 10 conversations (2 existing, 8 new)

## Aim

By the end of grade 4 a learner reads longer stories, plays, poems and informational texts of the grade on their own. They explain how a text of facts is organized and how its author supports a point, compare firsthand and secondhand accounts and two texts on one topic, read data in tables, and tell what a biography, a historical story and a tall tale are; in stories they explain why characters act, how the setting shapes events, what similes, metaphors, idioms and images mean, and how a play differs from a story. They use the progressive tenses, modal and helping verbs, prepositional phrases, the order of adjectives and reflexive pronouns, keep their tenses consistent, fix run-ons and comma splices, choose between commonly confused words and use quotation marks, abbreviations and formal English. They write summaries, cause-and-effect, narrative and descriptive paragraphs, reports of two paragraphs with headings, a persuasive letter, a tall tale, a short script, a response to a work of art and a paragraph of biography. They know the words of ten themes: the changing Earth, the biomes of the world, the human body, energy, tall tales, the Middle Ages, the Silk Road, art, the theater and lives that made a difference, and the Latin and Greek roots that build many of them.

## How to Read This Outline

Every unit is a theme and teaches four things together: the words of the theme, grammar and mechanics, reading skills, and writing. It ends with a reading unit on the theme and a conversation. Topics marked *existing* are in the graph already, in the band course `english-grades-3-5`, and keep their ids and their own prerequisites; each new topic lists its prerequisites, which may lie in an earlier unit or an earlier grade. A new topic of words lists the words it teaches, none of which another batch of the native courses teaches; any other new topic says what it teaches and its knowledge points as steps. A judged writing topic names its task and the criteria of its rubric, marking the criteria that are new.

Strands: of the 114 topics, 44 teach words (39%), 22 grammar and mechanics (19%), 23 reading (20%), 15 writing checked by rules (13%) and 10 writing judged by a model (9%).

Depth: each new grammar, reading or rule-checked writing knowledge point has 30 to 48 items built from hand-written cores by templates; each word of a vocabulary batch appears in three sentences, so each vocabulary knowledge point has 30 items; each judged writing knowledge point has 12 tasks with a model; reading units have 8 to 10 passages of 140 to 260 words, with three questions each. A batch of ten words earns about 15 XP, a word-study, grammar, reading or rule-checked writing topic about 14 and a judged writing topic about 22, so the lessons of the grade come to about 1,640 XP.

Listening, speaking and phonics wait for audio and recording: until then a unit lists its conversation but does not offer it, and each unit says what it will gain.

The band course `english-grades-3-5` retires once `english-grade-3`, `english-grade-4` and `english-grade-5` hold all of its topics. Its 78 topics are divided among the three grades, each in exactly one, with its id unchanged: 45 in grade 3, 21 in grade 4 and 12 in grade 5.

**Words:** `words-of-plays-and-theater` Words of Plays and Theater (after `context-clues`): script, scene, cast, rehearse, costume, prop, director, audition, curtain, spotlight; `words-of-comedy-and-drama` Words of Comedy and Drama (after `context-clues`): comedy, drama, heroine, suspense, climax, comic, intermission, encore, role, ovation; `words-for-speaking` Words for Speaking (after `context-clues`): announce, declare, reply, mention, suggest, insist, exclaim, complain, pronounce, interrupt. `using-a-thesaurus` Using a Thesaurus (after `synonyms-and-antonyms`, `using-a-dictionary-and-glossary`): A thesaurus lists words of similar meaning, which differ in strength and use. Steps: choosing the synonym from an entry that fits a sentence; then rejecting a synonym that has the wrong shade of meaning.

**Grammar:** `choosing-punctuation-for-effect` Choosing Punctuation for Effect (after `four-kinds-of-sentences`): A writer chooses an exclamation mark for strong feeling, a question mark for doubt, and a dash or an ellipsis for a pause or an unfinished thought. Steps: choosing the mark that gives a line a given effect; then choosing the effect a mark gives. `formal-and-informal-english` Formal and Informal English (after `contractions`): People speak informally with friends and formally in a report, a speech or a letter to a stranger: full forms, no slang, polite requests. Steps: telling a formal sentence from an informal one; then choosing the version that fits a situation.

**Reading:** `parts-of-a-play` Parts of a Play (after `parts-of-stories-and-poems`): A play has a cast of characters, a setting, scenes, dialogue after each speaker's name and stage directions in brackets. Steps: naming a part of a script; then choosing what a stage direction tells an actor. `poem-play-or-story` Poem, Play or Story (after `parts-of-a-play`): Poems have lines, stanzas, rhythm and rhyme; plays have dialogue and stage directions; stories have a narrator and paragraphs. Steps: naming the kind of a text from its features; then explaining which feature shows it. `story-and-script` A Story and Its Script (after `parts-of-a-play`, `retelling-key-events`): When a story becomes a script, the narrator's words become dialogue and stage directions. Steps: choosing the stage direction that matches a sentence of narration; then choosing the line of dialogue that matches what a narrator said. `what-characters-say-and-think` What Characters Say and Think (after `characters`, `character-change`): What a character says, does and thinks, and what others say about them, show who they are. Steps: choosing the trait a line of dialogue shows; then choosing the line that shows a given trait.

**Writing:** `script-format` Script Format (after `parts-of-a-play`, `punctuating-dialogue`): In a script each line starts with the speaker's name and a colon, without quotation marks, and stage directions go in brackets. Steps: turning a line of dialogue from a story into script format; then fixing the format of a script. `writing-a-short-script` Writing a Short Script (judged by a model; after `script-format`, `writing-a-narrative-paragraph`): A one-scene script for two or three characters, in script format, with a problem, dialogue that shows who the characters are and stage directions that help the actors. Criteria: `g4-script-format` *(new)*, `g4-script-conflict` *(new)*, `g4-script-dialogue` *(new)*, `g4-script-directions` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `plays-for-the-stage` Plays for the Stage *(new)*, 8 passages: The Lost Ticket; Grandpa's Robot; The Queen Who Hated Rain; The Lunch Swap; The Talent Show; Three Wishes Too Many; The Night the Lights Went Out; The Dragon's Dentist.

**Conversation:** `school-play-casting` Choosing roles for the school play *(new)*. The tutor is the teacher directing the school play. Task: say which role you want, explain what the character is like and why you would play it well.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a scene performed and saying how the actors' voices show feelings; reading a part aloud in a scene with the tutor.

### 10. Lives That Made a Difference

**Words:** `academic-words-2`, `suffixes-that-change-word-class` *(existing)*. `suffixes-for-people` Suffixes for People (after `common-suffixes`): The suffixes *-ist*, *-ian*, *-or*, *-er*, *-eer* and *-ant* make a word for a person who does something (*scientist*, *historian*, *inventor*, *pioneer*, *assistant*). Steps: choosing the person-word for an activity; then choosing what a person-word means. `words-for-courage-and-achievement` Words for Achievement (after `context-clues`): achieve, overcome, obstacle, accomplish, ambition, dedicate, honor, legacy, pioneer, struggle; `words-of-discovery-and-research` Words of Discovery and Research (after `context-clues`): research, investigate, theory, prove, examine, specimen, laboratory, breakthrough, accurate, publish; `words-for-helping-others` Words for Helping Others (after `context-clues`): volunteer, donate, charity, assist, cooperate, contribute, equal, justice, campaign, protest.

**Grammar:** `quotation-marks-for-quotations` Quotation Marks for Quotations (after `punctuating-dialogue`): Words copied exactly from a person or a text go in quotation marks, with a comma after the signal phrase (*Braille wrote, "..."*). Steps: choosing the sentence that marks a quotation correctly; then telling a quotation from a paraphrase, which takes no marks. `abbreviations` Abbreviations (after `capitalizing-names-and-titles`): Titles, days, months and places have short forms with a capital and a period (*Dr.*, *Mon.*, *Feb.*, *St.*), and some are written in capitals without periods (*USA*, *NASA*). Steps: choosing the abbreviation of a word; then choosing the word an abbreviation stands for. `tricky-verb-pairs` Tricky Verb Pairs (after `simple-verb-tenses`): *Lie* (rest) and *lay* (put), *sit* and *set*, *rise* and *raise*: the second of each pair takes an object. Steps: choosing the verb of a pair that fits a sentence; then choosing its past form (*lay*, *laid*; *rose*, *raised*).

**Reading:** `biography-or-autobiography` Biography and Autobiography (after `point-of-view`, `fiction-and-nonfiction`): A biography tells a person's life in the third person; an autobiography is told by the person in the first person. Steps: telling which a passage is; then choosing what each can tell that the other cannot. `adages-and-proverbs` Adages and Proverbs (after `idioms`): A proverb is a short old saying that gives advice (*Practice makes perfect*, *Where there's a will, there's a way*). Steps: choosing what a proverb means; then choosing the proverb that fits a situation in a life story.

**Writing:** `listing-sources` Listing Sources (after `capitalizing-names-and-titles`): A report lists the books and websites it used, with the author, the title and the year. Steps: choosing the information a source entry needs; then putting entries in alphabetical order by author. `writing-a-biography-paragraph` Writing a Paragraph of Biography (judged by a model; after `writing-a-paragraph`, `biography-or-autobiography`, `turning-notes-into-sentences`): A paragraph from notes about a person of the unit: who they were, the key events of their life in order, facts from the notes and why their work still matters. Criteria: `g4-bio-person` *(new)*, `g4-bio-order` *(new)*, `g4-bio-facts` *(new)*, `g4-bio-importance` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `lives-that-made-a-difference` Lives That Made a Difference *(new)*, 8 passages: Mary Anning, Fossil Hunter; Zhang Heng and the Earthquake Detector; Louis Braille's Dots; Marie Curie in Her Laboratory; Wangari Maathai Plants Trees; Tu Youyou and the Malaria Cure; Ibn al-Haytham and Light; Katherine Johnson Counts the Way to the Moon.

**Conversation:** `biography-interview` Interviewing a scientist from history *(new)*. The tutor plays a scientist from history. Task: interview her about one discovery: how she made it, the obstacle she faced and what it changed.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a short radio biography and putting the events of a life in order; giving a one-minute talk about a person aloud.

## What the Next Grade Builds On

Grade 5 builds on this year: the two-paragraph report grows into a report of three paragraphs and an opinion piece with one paragraph per reason, and the narrative into a mystery and a personal narrative with pacing; grammar moves to the perfect tenses, correlative conjunctions, commas after introductions and in series, and shifts of tense and person; reading turns to text structures, fact and opinion, the author's purpose, several accounts of one event and quoting a text accurately; and the themes go on to matter, cells, space, ancient Greece, the Americas, engineering and the past told in letters and diaries.
