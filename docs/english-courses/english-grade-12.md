# English, Grade 12

> This is Great Academy's own outline. Its design is in the [English curriculum](../english-curriculum.md) §6.3 and the [development plan](../english-development-plan.md) §E8. [简体中文](english-grade-12.zh-CN.md).

- **Course:** `english-grade-12`
- **Level:** Grade 12, for learners whose first language is English or who read English as well as a native speaker of their age
- **Prerequisite(s):** `english-grade-11`, or reading and writing arguments, syntheses of sources, critiques and analyses of motifs at the level of grade 11
- **Language of explanation:** English
- **Size:** 7 units · 35 topics (4 existing, 31 new: 14 of vocabulary, 4 of grammar and mechanics, 5 of reading, 3 of writing checked by rules, 5 of writing judged by a model) · about 580 XP · 6 reading units of 48 passages (1 existing, 5 new) · 7 conversations (1 existing, 6 new)

## Aim

By the end of grade 12 a learner reads the personal essays, investigative reports, writing on philosophy, criticism of art, translations and writing on risk of the grade on their own and writes about them in essays. They follow a thought experiment to its conclusion, evaluate a review, compare two translations of one passage and read claims of risk and probability with care. They mend ambiguous pronoun reference, use inverted conditionals, replace jargon with plain language and keep apart an eighth set of commonly confused words. They choose precise diction, define a key term for an argument and explain a risk clearly; and they write a personal essay, a college application essay, a profile of a person of conscience, a philosophical essay, a review of a work of art, a comparison of translations and a proposal to reduce a risk. They know the words of reflection, of power and conscience, philosophy and belief, art and taste, translation and world literature, and risk and crisis, and the last Latin and Greek roots and two more sets of academic words.

## How to Read This Outline

Every unit is a theme and teaches four things together: the words of the theme, grammar and mechanics, reading skills, and writing. It ends with a reading unit on the theme and a conversation, except the last, which has a conversation but no reading unit. Topics marked *existing* are in the graph already, in the band course `english-grades-9-12`, and keep their ids and their own prerequisites; each new topic lists its prerequisites, which may lie in an earlier unit or an earlier grade. A new topic of words lists the words it teaches, none of which another batch of the native courses teaches; any other new topic says what it teaches and its knowledge points as steps. A judged writing topic names its task and the criteria of its rubric, marking the criteria that are new.

Strands: of the 35 topics, 15 teach words (43%), 4 grammar and mechanics (11%), 6 reading (17%), 3 writing checked by rules (9%) and 7 writing judged by a model (20%).

Depth: each new grammar, reading or rule-checked writing knowledge point has 30 to 48 items built from hand-written cores by templates; each word of a vocabulary batch appears in three sentences, so each vocabulary knowledge point has 30 items; each judged writing knowledge point has 12 tasks with a model; new reading units have 8 passages of 300 to 600 words, with three questions each. A batch of ten words earns about 15 XP, a word-study, grammar, reading or rule-checked writing topic about 14 and a judged writing topic about 24, and the existing topics keep their own XP, so the lessons of the grade come to about 580 XP.

Listening and speaking wait for audio and recording: until then a unit lists its conversation but does not offer it, and each unit says what it will gain.

The band course `english-grades-9-12` retires once `english-grade-9` to `english-grade-12` hold all of its topics. Its 37 topics are divided among the four grades by its units, each in exactly one, with its id unchanged: 11 in grade 9 (the writer's craft, tragedy and the stage, and sentence variety for effect), 10 in grade 10 (voices of persuasion and poems across time), 12 in grade 11 (rights and laws, a century of change, and science and society) and 4 in grade 12 (essays of experience). Its reading units and conversations go with their units.

### 1. Essays of Experience

**Reading:** `reading-personal-essays` *(existing)*.

**Writing:** `writing-a-personal-essay`, `writing-a-college-application-essay` *(existing)*.

**Reading unit:** `essays-of-experience` *(existing)*, 8 passages: The Sweater I Lost; What a Personal Essay Does; Learning My Father's Language; The Race I Did Not Win; The Kitchen Table; The Year I Stopped Talking; A Letter to My Younger Self; The Night Shift at the Diner.

**Conversation:** `college-admissions-interview` *(existing)*. Task: In a college admissions interview, answer questions about yourself with specific examples, and ask one question of your own about the university.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a personal essay read aloud and naming the moment it turns; answering questions in a college admissions interview.

### 2. Power and Conscience

**Words:** `words-of-power-and-corruption` Words of Power and Corruption (after `context-clues`): graft, kickback, nepotism, cronyism, embezzle, extortion, malfeasance, impunity, patronage, sinecure; `words-of-conscience-and-duty` Words of Conscience and Duty (after `context-clues`): probity, rectitude, forthright, principled, misgiving, incorruptible, upright, abnegation, dutiful, unimpeachable.

**Grammar:** `ambiguous-pronoun-reference` Ambiguous Pronoun Reference (after `pronoun-antecedent-agreement`): Pronouns that could point to two nouns, or to none at all, and how to revise them so the reader knows who or what is meant. Steps: Is the Reference Clear; then The Revision That Is Clear.

**Reading:** `reading-an-investigative-report` Reading an Investigative Report (after `tracing-claims-to-sources`): How investigative reporters build a case from documents, named witnesses and unnamed sources, and how a reader weighs each kind of evidence. Steps: What Kind of Evidence; then What Would Confirm the Claim.

**Writing:** `choosing-precise-diction` Choosing Precise Diction (after `connotation-and-precise-words`, `formal-style`): Replacing vague words such as thing, good, nice and very with words that say exactly what is meant, with the right connotation for the purpose. Steps: Is the Word Right; then The Precise Word. `writing-a-profile-of-a-person-of-conscience` Writing a Profile of a Person of Conscience (judged by a model; after `reading-an-investigative-report`, `choosing-precise-diction`): Write a profile that turns the facts about a person into a story centered on a moral choice, its cost, and why it matters to others. Criteria: `g12-profile-choice` *(new)*, `g12-profile-detail` *(new)*, `g12-profile-meaning` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `power-and-corruption` Power and Corruption *(new)*, 8 passages: The Reporter and the Oil Company; The Doctor Who Said No; The Laboratory of Obedience; The Company That Hid Its Debts; A Historian's Warning; The Secret History of a War; The Policeman Who Would Not Take Money; The Judge Who Took No Inkstone.

**Conversation:** `reporting-wrongdoing` Should you report it? *(new)*. Task: You have found evidence that someone in a club you belong to has been taking money meant for a charity. Explain what you found and how sure you are, weigh the duty to report against loyalty to the group, decide whom to tell first, and answer a friend who asks you to stay quiet.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a whistleblower's account and separating evidence from accusation; deciding with a friend whether to report wrongdoing.

### 3. Belief and Doubt

**Words:** `words-of-philosophy` Words of Philosophy (after `context-clues`): metaphysics, epistemology, ontology, empiricism, rationalism, dualism, determinism, materialism, stoicism, utilitarianism; `words-of-belief-and-doubt` Words of Belief and Doubt (after `context-clues`): dogma, dogmatic, agnostic, orthodoxy, heterodox, zealot, certitude, apostate, creed, devout.

**Grammar:** `inverted-conditionals` Inverted Conditionals (after `conditional-sentences`, `the-subjunctive-in-wishes-and-conditions`): Conditions without if, made by putting had, were or should before the subject, as in Had I known, Were it true and Should you need help. Steps: Which Condition Is This; then The Inversion That Fits.

**Reading:** `following-a-thought-experiment` Following a Thought Experiment (after `reasoning`, `explaining-with-an-analogy`): How philosophers and scientists imagine a case that could not or need not happen, draw out an intuition about it, and reason from that intuition to a conclusion. Steps: Which Step Is This; then What the Case Is Meant to Show.

**Writing:** `defining-a-term-in-an-argument` Defining a Term in an Argument (after `facts-definitions-and-examples`, `qualifying-a-claim`): Stating what a key word such as freedom, success or fair means in an argument, so that the reader and the writer argue about the same thing. Steps: What Kind of Definition; then The Definition That Works. `writing-a-philosophical-essay` Writing a Philosophical Essay (judged by a model; after `following-a-thought-experiment`, `defining-a-term-in-an-argument`, `writing-a-critique-of-an-argument`): Write a short philosophical essay that defines its key term, takes a position, tests it with an example or thought experiment, and answers the strongest objection. Criteria: `g12-philosophy-definition` *(new)*, `g12-philosophy-case` *(new)*, `g12-philosophy-objection` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `questions-of-philosophy` Questions of Philosophy *(new)*, 8 passages: The Philosopher Who Doubted Everything; The Ring That Made Men Invisible; The Sunrise That May Not Come; Two Views of Human Nature; The Machine of Pleasure; The Veil of Ignorance; The Scientist in the Black and White Room; The Runaway Trolley.

**Conversation:** `philosophy-cafe` A question at the philosophy café *(new)*. Task: At a philosophy café, the question is whether you would plug into a machine that gives you any experience you want. Define what you mean by a good life, take a position, test it with an example, and answer the strongest objection the host raises.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a thought experiment and restating its premises; defending a position at a philosophy café.

### 4. Art and Its Critics

**Words:** `words-of-art-criticism` Words of Art Criticism (after `context-clues`): chiaroscuro, oeuvre, derivative, brushwork, figurative, iconography, impasto, installation, masterwork, tableau; `words-of-taste-and-beauty` Words of Taste and Beauty (after `context-clues`): exquisite, garish, tawdry, gaudy, understated, philistine, harmonious, picturesque, insipid, ornate.

**Reading:** `evaluating-a-review` Evaluating a Review (after `supported-and-unsupported-claims`, `connotation-and-precise-words`): How critics describe a work, judge it and give reasons, and how a reader tells a well-supported judgment from a mere opinion. Steps: What the Sentence Does; then The Best-Supported Judgment.

**Writing:** `writing-a-review-of-a-work-of-art` Writing a Review of a Work of Art (judged by a model; after `evaluating-a-review`, `choosing-precise-diction`): Write a review that describes a work briefly, judges it with reasons tied to specific features and their effects, weighs a weakness, and ends with a clear verdict. Criteria: `g12-review-description` *(new)*, `g12-review-reasons` *(new)*, `g12-review-verdict` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `art-and-its-critics` Art and Its Critics *(new)*, 8 passages: The Night of the Riot; The Critic Who Named a Movement; A Signed Piece of Plumbing; The Painting That Shredded Itself; The Nanny's Hidden Photographs; The Painter Who Sued a Critic; The Novel That Failed; The Stars of the Crowd.

**Conversation:** `modern-art-gallery-talk` A talk in the gallery *(new)*. Task: You are standing with a visitor in front of a work of modern art. Describe the work briefly, give your judgment with reasons tied to specific features, admit one weakness, and answer the visitor who says that a child could have made it.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a critic on a work of art and noting the features named; guiding a skeptical visitor in a gallery.

### 5. Literature Across Languages

**Words:** `words-of-translation-and-meaning` Words of Translation and Meaning (after `context-clues`): fidelity, literal, idiomatic, rendering, equivalent, untranslatable, gloss, polysemy, calque, transliterate; `words-of-world-literature` Words of World Literature (after `context-clues`): canon, anthology, picaresque, novella, bildungsroman, laureate, compendium, epigraph, fabulist, palimpsest.

**Reading:** `comparing-translations` Comparing Translations (after `connotation-and-precise-words`, `tone-and-mood`): How two translations of the same line differ in word choice, in sentence shape and rhythm, or in meaning added or lost, and what each difference does to the reader. Steps: What Differs; then What the Difference Does.

**Writing:** `writing-a-comparison-of-translations` Writing a Comparison of Translations (judged by a model; after `comparing-translations`, `writing-a-comparison-of-two-texts`): Write a short essay that compares two translations of the same line, quoting the words that differ, explaining what each difference does, and judging which serves the original better. Criteria: `g12-translation-claim` *(new)*, `g12-translation-evidence` *(new)*, `g12-translation-judgment` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `literature-across-languages` Literature Across Languages *(new)*, 8 passages: Three Difficulties of Translation; A Translation That Felt Foreign; The Poet Who Translated Himself; The Woman Who Brought Russian Novels into English; Three Versions of an Old Novel; The Man Who Rewrote a Classic; Machines That Translate; Words with No Translation.

**Conversation:** `translators-choice` The translator's choice *(new)*. Task: You are helping to translate a short poem or a saying from your first language into English. Explain what the original says and what makes it hard to translate, compare a literal and a freer version, and defend the one you would choose to an editor who prefers the other.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to two readings of one poem in translation and naming what differs; defending a translation to an editor.

### 6. Risk and Responsibility

**Words:** `words-of-risk-and-uncertainty` Words of Risk and Uncertainty (after `context-clues`): contingency, precaution, likelihood, volatile, precarious, actuarial, threshold, foreseeable, hedge, probabilistic; `words-of-crisis-and-response` Words of Crisis and Response (after `context-clues`): catastrophe, cataclysm, containment, mobilize, fallout, stopgap, rationing, escalate, cordon, onset.

**Grammar:** `jargon-and-plain-language` Jargon and Plain Language (after `reducing-nominalizations`, `euphemisms`): Specialist terms used where readers will not know them, inflated words that make simple things sound grand, and the plain words that say the same thing clearly. Steps: What Gets in the Way; then The Plain Version.

**Reading:** `reading-risk-and-probability` Reading Risk and Probability (after `how-numbers-can-mislead`, `reading-charts-and-statistics`): How reports state risks as absolute numbers, as relative changes or as single stories, and how to work out what a risk really means for a person. Steps: How the Risk Is Stated; then What the Number Really Means.

**Writing:** `explaining-a-risk-clearly` Explaining a Risk Clearly (after `reading-risk-and-probability`, `keeping-an-objective-tone`): Writing about a danger so that readers learn its real size: absolute numbers, a fair comparison and calm words, rather than alarm or false comfort. Steps: How the Risk Is Told; then The Clearest Statement. `writing-a-proposal-to-reduce-a-risk` Writing a Proposal to Reduce a Risk (judged by a model; after `explaining-a-risk-clearly`, `writing-an-editorial`): Write a proposal that states a risk in absolute numbers, recommends specific measures with their costs, and answers an objection in calm, persuasive words. Criteria: `g12-proposal-risk` *(new)*, `g12-proposal-plan` *(new)*, `g12-proposal-objection` *(new)*.

**Reading unit:** `risk-and-responsibility` Risk and Responsibility *(new)*, 8 passages: The Test That Went Wrong; The Wall That Was Too Low; The Fog That Killed; The Book About Unsafe Cars; The Bug That Did Not Bite; The Fear of Flying; A Principle of Caution; The Country That Decided Never to Flood Again.

**Conversation:** `town-meeting-on-risk` A meeting about a risk *(new)*. Task: Your town is debating whether to spend money on a defense against a rare danger, such as a flood or a fire. State the risk with numbers, propose one measure and its cost, compare the risk of acting with the risk of not acting, and answer a resident who says the money would be wasted.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a briefing on a risk and checking its numbers; speaking at a town meeting about a defense.

### 7. Roots and Parts

**Words:** `latin-roots-10` Latin Roots 10 (after `latin-roots-9`): Ten more Latin roots: cord, gress, plic, volv, sid, sign, dorm, jud, nunc and brev. Steps: The Meaning of the Latin root; then Choosing the Word from Its Parts. `greek-roots-9` Greek Roots 9 (after `greek-roots-8`): Ten more Greek roots: ped, agog, tax, top, tom, zo, odont, phras, stas and orama. Steps: The Meaning of the Greek root; then Choosing the Word from Its Parts. `academic-words-10` Academic Words 10 (after `context-clues`): Ten words of writing about what is basic, widespread and related: preclude, ascertain, concomitant, intrinsic and more. Steps: What They Mean; then Using Them; then Spelling Them. `academic-words-11` Academic Words 11 (after `context-clues`): Ten words of writing about claims, conditions and effects: contingent, efficacy, ameliorate, provisional and more. Steps: What They Mean; then Using Them; then Spelling Them.

**Grammar:** `commonly-confused-words-8` Commonly Confused Words 8 (after `commonly-confused-words-7`): Pairs such as ascent and assent, defuse and diffuse, or forbear and forebear look or sound alike but mean different things. Steps: The Word That Fits; then The Word for the Meaning.

**Conversation:** `word-detective` Word detectives *(new)*. Task: A younger student is stuck on long words in a science and history text. Break three words into Latin or Greek roots and parts, explain how the parts build the meaning, and give a tip for guessing words that cannot be split cleanly.

**Later, with audio and recording:** Listening to a talk on the roots of words and noting the parts it names; tutoring a younger student on long words.

## What the Next Grade Builds On

`english-college-writing` builds on this year: the personal and philosophical essays grow into academic essays and research papers, and reading for argument, translation and risk goes on to scholarly sources.
