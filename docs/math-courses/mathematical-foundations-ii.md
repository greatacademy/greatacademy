# Mathematical Foundations II

> Source: <https://www.mathacademy.com/courses/mathematical-foundations-ii> (retrieved 2026-10-30). Part of the [math curriculum catalog](../math-curriculum.md).

- **Level:** Mathematical Foundations (adults)
- **Prerequisite(s):** Mathematical Foundations I
- **Size:** 14 units · 52 modules · 360 topics

## Description

Our Mathematical Foundations II course is the second stage of Math Academy's accelerated Mathematical Foundations sequence, designed to take motivated adults from fractions to calculus, preparing them for university-level mathematics.

Building on the algebraic and geometric foundations established in the first course, students progress through quadratics, polynomials, complex numbers, advanced functions, logarithms, trigonometry, vectors, limits, and the fundamentals of calculus. This course develops the mathematical fluency and conceptual understanding required to move beyond high school mathematics and into university-level topics.

Designed for adult learners pursuing a rigorous and efficient path toward advanced mathematics, the course emphasizes algebraic techniques, function analysis, mathematical reasoning, and the connections between symbolic and visual representations. Students learn to analyze increasingly sophisticated functions, solve nonlinear equations, work with trigonometric relationships, interpret limits, calculate derivatives, and find elementary antiderivatives.

Upon completing this course, students will have reached introductory calculus and be prepared for the more advanced calculus, linear algebra, and university-level mathematics developed in Mathematical Foundations III.

## Overview

The course covers quadratics, including solving quadratic equations using factoring, completing the square, the quadratic formula, analyzing the discriminant, applying relationships between roots and coefficients, graphing and transforming quadratic functions, determining vertices, axes of symmetry, roots, domains, and ranges, solving nonlinear systems, finding intersections between lines and parabolas, and modeling vertical motion.

Higher-degree polynomial topics include analyzing polynomial structure, factoring higher-order expressions, solving cubic and higher-degree polynomial equations, performing synthetic and long division, applying the factor, remainder, and rational roots theorems, determining root multiplicities, and graphing polynomial functions using their roots, degree, leading coefficient, and end behavior.

The study of number systems includes distinguishing rational and irrational numbers, working with imaginary and complex numbers, performing arithmetic with complex numbers, solving quadratic equations with complex roots, using the cyclic properties of the imaginary unit, and representing complex numbers geometrically in the complex plane using magnitude and argument.

In functions, students work with function arithmetic and composition, analyze local extrema, identify one-to-one and invertible functions, calculate inverses, interpret inverse graphs, study periodic and even or odd functions, investigate unbounded behavior and asymptotes, and transform graphs using translations, stretches, compressions, and reflections.

The course covers exponentials and logarithms, along with radical and rational functions. Students convert between exponential and logarithmic forms, apply logarithm laws, solve exponential and logarithmic equations, analyze transformed exponential and logarithmic graphs, simplify rational expressions, solve rational equations, identify rational-function roots and asymptotes, simplify radicals, solve radical equations, and analyze transformed square root and cube root functions.

Work with sequences includes defining recursive and explicit sequences, working with arithmetic and geometric sequences, using sigma notation, and analyzing finite sums. In geometry, students apply coordinate methods, distance and midpoint formulas, geometric transformations, congruence, similarity, and three-dimensional measurements involving polyhedra, cylinders, spheres, and other solids.

Trigonometry topics include solving right triangles using trigonometric ratios, applying the law of sines and law of cosines, working with radian measure, using the unit circle to extend trigonometry beyond acute angles, evaluating special trigonometric ratios, graphing all six trigonometric functions, analyzing their properties, and transforming trigonometric graphs. Students also study vectors, including vector addition, scalar multiplication, magnitudes, unit vectors, linear combinations, and two-dimensional Cartesian representations.

The course introduces the concepts that underpin calculus through limits and continuity. Students estimate limits from graphs, work with one-sided limits and limits at infinity, apply algebraic limit laws, evaluate limits involving different function families, and determine continuity at points and over intervals.

In introductory calculus, students connect average rates of change to instantaneous rates of change, define derivatives, apply differentiation rules to algebraic, exponential, logarithmic, and trigonometric functions, find tangent and normal lines, calculate higher-order derivatives, differentiate composite functions using the chain rule, and find elementary antiderivatives.

Statistics, probability, and combinatorics are also covered. Students calculate measures of centrality and spread, analyze covariance and z-scores, interpret scatter plots, trend lines, correlation, regression, and residuals, and develop probability reasoning using sample spaces, events, complements, unions, intersections, counting principles, permutations, and combinations.
