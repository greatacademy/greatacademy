## Description

Our Mathematical Foundations III course completes Math Academy's three-course journey from fractions to calculus, preparing ambitious adult learners for university-level mathematics.

Building on the algebra, functions, trigonometry, and introductory calculus developed in the earlier courses, students progress through sequences and series, advanced inequalities, parametric and polar coordinates, conic sections, complex numbers, rigorous function analysis, differentiation, integration, differential equations, vectors, linear algebra, and probability. This course emphasizes mathematical fluency, analytical reasoning, sophisticated problem-solving techniques, and the ability to connect abstract mathematical ideas with their applications.

Designed for motivated adults who want to reach an advanced level of mathematical proficiency efficiently, the course develops the tools needed for further study in mathematics, physics, engineering, computer science, and other quantitative disciplines. Students deepen their understanding of calculus through derivatives, definite integrals, integration techniques, optimization, motion, and differential equations, while extending their mathematical toolkit through vector methods, matrices, and probability distributions.

Upon completing this course, students will have progressed through the full Mathematical Foundations sequence—from essential arithmetic to university-level calculus and beyond—and will be prepared for more advanced mathematical study.

## Overview

The course covers sequences and series, including applying the binomial theorem, working with Pascal's triangle and binomial coefficients, calculating sums of arithmetic and geometric series, using sigma notation, and investigating infinite series. Students analyze convergence and divergence, calculate partial sums, and determine when infinite geometric series have finite sums.

The study of inequalities involves solving quadratic, polynomial, rational, radical, exponential, and logarithmic inequalities using algebraic, graphical, and sign-table methods. Students also solve inequalities involving geometric sequences, graph two-variable linear inequalities and systems of inequalities, and analyze nonlinear inequalities and regions in the coordinate plane.

In parametric and polar coordinates, students represent curves parametrically, convert between parametric and Cartesian equations, find intersections, differentiate parametric curves, determine tangent and normal lines, and calculate arc lengths. They also convert between polar and Cartesian coordinates, analyze polar equations of circles and lines, differentiate polar curves, and calculate areas and arc lengths in polar form.

Conic sections include the equations and properties of circles, parabolas, ellipses, and hyperbolas. Students use algebraic and graphical methods to identify centers, vertices, axes, intercepts, intersections, and asymptotes. They also work with parametric representations of conic sections and use completing the square to determine geometric properties from equations.

The course develops advanced trigonometry. Students graph and evaluate inverse trigonometric functions, solve trigonometric equations, determine general solutions, manipulate trigonometric identities, and apply sum, difference, and double-angle formulas. They also represent combinations of sine and cosine functions in amplitude-phase form.

Work with complex numbers includes complex conjugates, complex division, equating real and imaginary parts, polar representations, Euler's formula, De Moivre's theorem, roots of unity, and applications of the fundamental theorem of algebra to polynomial equations with complex roots.

Limits and continuity are covered at an advanced level. Students apply L'Hôpital's rule, evaluate limits of sequences and functions, analyze asymptotic behavior, use special trigonometric and exponential limits, and apply the intermediate value theorem.

Differentiation topics include differentiating implicit and inverse functions, applying the mean value theorem, locating critical points, analyzing increasing and decreasing intervals, classifying extrema, investigating concavity and points of inflection, and using linearization. Students also estimate derivatives numerically and approximate functions using Taylor polynomials, Maclaurin series, and power series.

The study of definite integrals involves approximating areas using Riemann sums and the trapezoidal rule, defining definite integrals, applying the fundamental theorem of calculus, calculating areas bounded by curves, interpreting integrals as accumulation functions, and solving problems involving average values and arc lengths.

Advanced integration techniques include substitution, trigonometric identities, polynomial division, completing the square, integration by parts, and partial fractions. Students also analyze improper integrals over unbounded intervals and near singularities.

The course includes contextual applications of calculus. Students calculate displacement, velocity, and acceleration for straight-line and planar motion; use differentiation and integration to analyze moving objects; solve related-rates problems; and use derivatives to optimize quantities and distances.

Differential equations topics include verifying solutions, solving first-order ordinary differential equations using direct integration and separation of variables, solving initial-value problems, interpreting differential equations qualitatively, constructing mathematical models, and approximating solutions using Euler's method.

In vectors and linear algebra, students work with three-dimensional Cartesian vectors, dot products, projections, cross products, scalar triple products, and vector-valued functions. They also study matrix arithmetic and multiplication, determinants, inverse matrices, systems of linear equations, and linear transformations, including their geometric interpretations and compositions.

Probability topics include conditional probability, the multiplication and addition laws, the law of total probability, independence, and mutually exclusive events. Students also analyze discrete random variables, probability mass functions, cumulative distributions, expected values, binomial and geometric distributions, and the normal distribution, using probability models to solve mathematical and real-world problems.
